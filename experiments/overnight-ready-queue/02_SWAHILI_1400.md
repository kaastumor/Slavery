# Swahili maritime trade network — 1400

Target: R1:N:swahili_1400. Frozen frame: **mobile_network**.  
Research stage: **researched_internal**.  
Research outcome: **bounded-supported medieval slave-trade participation with node-specific slavery/practice evidence; network-wide territorial prevalence unresolved**.  
Overnight ready-queue Stage 2 under issue #262; no R1 reviewed-state change,
canonical update, P-level, geometry promotion or publication.

## Bounded synthesis

The evidence supports a positive claim that the medieval Swahili maritime system around
the 1400 horizon participated in the movement and exchange of enslaved people.

The strongest support comes from three complementary evidence types:

1. specialist longue-durée synthesis: Thomas Vernet-Habasque describes the Swahili
   coast from c. 900–1500 as a **steady but not massive exporter of slaves**, mainly
   toward the Gulf and southern Arabia;
2. near-anchor maritime/customs evidence: Fleisher et al. synthesize Rasulid-Yemeni
   evidence showing regular eastern African maritime trade in the thirteenth and
   fourteenth centuries, with slaves included among commodities reaching Arabia and
   with late-fourteenth-century descriptions of ships from Sawahil towns feeding
   Mogadishu/Barawa and onward Red Sea/Arabian networks;
3. bounded local evidence: Ibn Battuta's 1331 account of Kilwa records enslaved people
   at the sultan's court and frequent raids into neighboring Zanj country, while
   fifteenth-century Songo Mnara archaeology supports the likely presence and labor of
   enslaved people at one Swahili stone town in the Kilwa archipelago.

Taken together, this supports **network participation in slavery/slave trading around
1400**.

It does not support:
- one uniform Swahili polity;
- one coast-wide territorial slavery regime;
- uniform local slaveholding at every port;
- a large-volume export economy comparable to the eighteenth/nineteenth centuries;
- a quantified prevalence estimate;
- a claim that every cargo or every trade route carried enslaved people.

The bounded conclusion is therefore:

**Around 1400, the Swahili maritime network participated in a persistent but
comparatively modest medieval slave trade, with concrete node-level slavery evidence
at Kilwa and bounded fifteenth-century archaeological evidence at Songo Mnara.
Network participation is supported; coast-wide territorial practice, uniformity and
scale remain unresolved.**

No P-level is assigned.

## Frame and chronology

EXP-07 qualified this target as a **mobile_network** rather than a polity.

That distinction is load-bearing.

The relevant historical object is a set of interconnected coastal towns, ports,
islands, maritime routes and interior-facing exchange links, not a single jurisdiction.

The 1400 anchor is supported by:
- late-fourteenth-century Rasulid/Yemeni descriptions of regular Swahili maritime
  movement;
- specialist synthesis treating slave export as persistent through c. 900–1500;
- Songo Mnara's fifteenth-century occupation and slavery-focused archaeological
  interpretation;
- Tumbatu/Mkokotoni occupation extending to c. 1400, with a 2025/2026 archaeological
  study exploring enslaved/migrant identities there.

The project does not force these materials into one exact-day or exact-year event.
The temporal designation is **near-anchor network continuity with node-specific
evidence on either side of 1400**.

## External/network participation

Vernet-Habasque's 2024 specialist synthesis is the strongest interpretive backbone.

It explicitly distinguishes the medieval pattern from later plantation-era systems:
- the medieval coast participated in slave trading over the long term;
- export was steady but not massive;
- demand was especially connected to the Gulf and southern Arabia;
- dependence on the coast was heterogeneous, with chattel slavery only one form among
  broader clientship/dependency relations;
- nineteenth-century scale should not be projected backward.

This supports the network-level proposition while constraining scale.

Fleisher et al. add near-anchor maritime structure. Their synthesis of Rasulid-Yemeni
customs and related evidence states that thirteenth/fourteenth-century trade from
Mogadishu and the southern Swahili coast regularly linked eastern Africa to Arabian
ports and included enslaved people among exported commodities.

A late-fourteenth-century witness, Qadi Masud at Aden, is cited for ships from
Sawahil towns feeding northern collection/trans-shipment points and onward trade to
Aden/Hadramawt.

This demonstrates that the slavery evidence belongs inside a genuine maritime network
rather than being only isolated local possession.

It still does not prove that every Swahili town exported slaves or that every route
carried them.

## Kilwa — concrete local slavery and raiding near the anchor

Ibn Battuta's 1331 account supplies a bounded primary-source locus.

At Kilwa:
- the sultan is described as making repeated raids into neighboring Zanj country;
- enslaved people appear as transferable gifts at court;
- twenty enslaved people are given in one reported episode.

This supports:
- local possession of enslaved people at Kilwa;
- alienability/transfer of enslaved people;
- raiding/captive-taking associated with the Kilwa ruler.

It does not independently establish:
- the origin of all enslaved people at Kilwa;
- the scale of export;
- coast-wide practice;
- a uniform legal status across Swahili settlements.

The teaching edition used here reproduces Freeman-Grenville's English translation of
Ibn Battuta. It is one textual chain, not multiple independent witnesses.

## Songo Mnara — fifteenth-century node evidence

Rødland, Wynne-Jones, Wood and Fleisher's 2020 archaeological study explicitly
investigates enslaved laborers at fifteenth-century Songo Mnara.

The authors use the distribution of terracotta and shell beads as possible proxies for
lower-status/enslaved people and argue that the site's domestic and productive spaces
are compatible with the presence and activities of enslaved workers.

This is important positive evidence, but the material proxies are not uniquely
diagnostic.

The appropriate use is:
- support a bounded site-level slavery interpretation;
- preserve uncertainty in the material signature;
- do not turn one site into a coast-wide prevalence claim.

This source is already represented elsewhere in the repository as a reviewed
site-bounded Songo Mnara case. Here it is reused with explicit dependency lineage,
not counted as a newly independent discovery.

## Tumbatu and Mkokotoni — exact-horizon archaeological context

Rødland's 2025/2026 Cambridge Archaeological Journal study examines Tumbatu and
Mkokotoni in northern Zanzibar, with occupation deposits dated broadly c. 1000–1400.

The paper argues that enslaved and non-elite migrants may be visible through outlier
pottery traditions and situates those sites within medieval Swahili systems of trade,
migration, dependency and slavery.

The study is explicit about the limits:
- material evidence does not prove the status of individual potters;
- the medieval scale of slavery cannot be reliably quantified;
- slavery and migration overlap but are not interchangeable;
- local dependence varied.

This provides useful exact-horizon pressure against both overclaim and underclaim:
slavery belongs in the social/economic interpretation of the network, but status and
scale remain difficult to resolve archaeologically.

Required mapping:

- slave-trade participation ≠ local territorial prevalence;
- enslaved migrant ≠ all migrants;
- client/dependent ≠ slave by default;
- archaeological low-status proxy ≠ proven slave status for every individual;
- Kilwa raiding ≠ coast-wide raiding;
- later plantation slavery ≠ medieval Swahili slavery.

## Law / practice

No single coast-wide legal regime is assigned.

Islamic legal and social norms are relevant to medieval Swahili slavery, but this packet
does not infer a uniform law across all towns from general Islamic doctrine.

The evidence supports practice and network participation at bounded nodes and in
specialist regional synthesis.

## Network / territory

This target specifically tests the distinction between **network participation** and
**territorial practice**.

Supported:
- enslaved people were part of the medieval Swahili/Indian Ocean exchange world;
- export from the coast around this period was persistent but modest;
- local slavery is directly attested at Kilwa and archaeologically supported at
  Songo Mnara;
- multiple ports and maritime links participated in a wider commercial system.

Withheld:
- a single Swahili territorial slavery polygon;
- a uniform coast-wide institution;
- equal participation by every node;
- one network-wide prevalence value.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci:
- Kilwa, 1331 primary textual witness;
- Rasulid/Yemeni maritime-customs and voyage evidence for thirteenth/fourteenth-century
  East African–Arabian exchange;
- Songo Mnara, fifteenth-century archaeological site;
- Tumbatu/Mkokotoni, c. 1000–1400 archaeological townscape;
- specialist synthesis of the Swahili coast c. 900–1500.

Maximum defensible inference:

**The Swahili maritime network around 1400 participated in a persistent medieval
slave trade and included nodes where enslaved people were locally present. The
network was heterogeneous, the trade was not demonstrated at later mass scale, and
local territorial prevalence cannot be generalized from individual nodes.**

Required abstention:
- no coast-wide P-level;
- no uniform practice polygon;
- no inferred participation for every port;
- no numerical scale estimate from source counts;
- no projection of Zanzibar's nineteenth-century plantation economy backward;
- no automatic equation of migration, clientship or low status with slavery.

- Vernet-Habasque 2024 is the principal specialist longue-durée synthesis and is used
  for classification/scale.
- Fleisher et al. 2015 synthesize Rasulid/Yemeni maritime evidence; their specific
  customs claims depend partly on Vallet's work and are not counted as an entirely
  independent primary archive.

  and is already represented elsewhere in the repository; reuse is recorded rather
  than double-counted.

- No source count is converted into prevalence or trade volume.

## Research disposition

**Bounded-supported for medieval network participation in slave trading around 1400,
with node-specific local slavery evidence; network-wide territorial prevalence and
scale unresolved.**

Preserve as supported:
- persistent medieval slave-export participation in the Swahili coast network;
- Kilwa 1331 local slavery and raiding/captive-taking;
- fifteenth-century Songo Mnara as a bounded archaeological slavery locus.

Preserve as limited/contextual:
- Tumbatu/Mkokotoni enslaved/migrant material-culture interpretation;
- Rasulid/customs evidence as network structure and commodity movement.

Do not promote as supported:
- one uniform coast-wide slavery regime;
- a mass export system comparable to later centuries;
- territorial practice at every Swahili port;
- quantified prevalence.

Reopen/deepen if:
- port-specific late-fourteenth/early-fifteenth-century records clarify particular
  routes or slave cargoes;
- additional independent site studies support or challenge local slavery at other
  Swahili nodes;
- direct Rasulid customs editions permit tighter source-native reconstruction.

## Adversarial findings

- **SUPPORTED:** medieval Swahili slave-trade participation around the 1400 horizon.
- **SUPPORTED:** enslaved people at Kilwa in 1331.
- **SUPPORTED:** Kilwa ruler's raiding/captive-taking in Ibn Battuta's account.
- **SUPPORTED WITH MATERIAL-PROXY LIMIT:** enslaved labor at fifteenth-century Songo

- **REJECT:** all Swahili towns participated equally.
- **REJECT:** maritime trade connectivity alone proves slave trading.
- **REJECT:** later Zanzibar plantation scale can be projected into 1400.
- **REJECT:** node-level slavery becomes a coast-wide territorial claim.

  node-level territorial practice remain separate.
