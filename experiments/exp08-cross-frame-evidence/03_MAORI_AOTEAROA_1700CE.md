# Māori communities in Aotearoa — 1700 CE

## Bounded synthesis

Specialist and institutional syntheses support **war-captive unfreedom as a real
institution in traditional/pre-contact Māori society**. The terms `taurekareka` and
`mōkai` are routinely mapped in English-language scholarship to captive/slave or
servant/slave statuses, and traditional-status syntheses describe captives taken from
enemy groups after warfare.

That supports a bounded positive result for the broader pre-contact social world that
contains the 1700 anchor.

It does **not** support one homogeneous Aotearoa-wide slavery regime at exactly 1700.

The frozen target is deliberately a retrospective regional/community frame over many
distinct iwi and hapū. Detailed documentation is strongest after European contact, and
the early nineteenth-century Musket Wars substantially changed the scale, movement and
economic use of captives. Those later conditions cannot be projected backward to 1700.

The bounded conclusion is therefore:

**war-captive unfreedom/slavery-category relations were part of traditional/pre-contact
Māori society applicable to the social world around the 1700 anchor, but their exact
distribution, prevalence, terminology and practice across individual iwi/hapū at 1700
remain unresolved. The result is positive at the period/institution level and explicitly
non-uniform at the frozen aggregate level.**

No P-level is assigned.

## Frame and chronology

“Māori” is a retrospective collective label for the frozen 1700 target. It must not be
treated as one polity, jurisdiction or culturally uniform community.

The relevant social units were whānau, hapū and iwi with their own leadership,
relationships, conflicts and histories. The target is therefore a comparative research
container, not a claim that every member community shared identical institutions.

The 1700 anchor predates sustained European documentary observation. The evidence is a
combination of:
- specialist synthesis using Māori oral traditions and colonial-era records;
- institutional reconstructions explicitly describing society before European contact;
- late eighteenth-century early-contact observations;
- later customary-law and ethnographic reconstruction.

This supports period-level continuity more strongly than exact-year distribution.

## War-captive status in traditional society

Hazel Petrie's *Outcasts of the Gods?* is the strongest specialist synthesis used in
this pass. It is explicitly a history of Māori war captives and analyses freedom and
unfreedom in traditional Māori society using Māori oral sources together with written
colonial records.

Te Ara's social-rank syntheses independently describe pre-European Māori society as
including `taurekareka` or `mōkai` among recognized social ranks and describe
`taurekareka` as people from enemy groups captured in battle.

This evidence is sufficient to reject a blanket “no pre-contact captive/slave status”
position.

It is not sufficient to assign one uniform status system to every iwi/hapū or to count
the proportion of people in such statuses at 1700.

## Captivity after warfare

Te Ara's synthesis of traditional Māori warfare describes victors taking prisoners
after battle, especially women and children, with some becoming slaves; other outcomes
included killing, intermarriage and group merger.

The variation matters. Captivity was one possible outcome of warfare, not a mechanical
consequence of every conflict.

An early-contact counterexample reinforces that boundary. Hawkesworth's published 1773
account of Cook's first voyage inferred from one recent conflict that the people
observed gave no quarter and took no prisoners. That observer conclusion cannot overturn
the broader specialist record, but it is valuable evidence against universalizing a
single post-battle pattern across Aotearoa.

Accordingly:
- warfare ≠ automatic captive-taking;
- captive-taking ≠ identical status in every community;
- one observer's “no prisoners” episode ≠ absence of a broader captive institution.

## Category mapping: taurekareka, mōkai and slavery

English-language syntheses often render `taurekareka` as “slave,” but the mapping
requires caution.

The Waitangi Tribunal's Rangahaua Whānui customary-land-tenure report explicitly warns
that translating `taurekareka` as “slave” is not entirely accurate. Its discussion of
conquest also distinguishes situations in which defeated survivors remained on land as
`taurekareka`, situations involving service, and arrangements that did not amount to
“absolute serfdom.”

Te Ara further distinguishes:
- `taurekareka` associated with enemy captives;
- dependent hapū described through the category `tāngata`, sometimes glossed as
  “serfs,” who owed tribute/allegiance to stronger hapū;
- rangatira and tūtūā/commoner ranks.

These categories must not be collapsed.

`Mōkai` is also semantically broader than one English legal category and may be
translated as servant, captive or slave depending on context. A term occurrence alone
therefore does not prove a chattel-slavery relation.

The Atlas should preserve the source-native term and map it claim by claim.

## Labor roles

Te Ara's reconstruction of daily life in traditional communities describes women and
slaves carrying water, firewood and prepared food, citing Anne Salmond's *Two Worlds:
First Meetings between Māori and Europeans, 1642–1772*.

This is useful for the social/labor role attached to captive/slave categories in the
early-contact reconstruction. It is not an exact 1700 census and should not be used to
infer prevalence or identical labor allocation across every community.

Adele Fletcher's doctoral study of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century documentary
sources likewise treats distinctions among rangatira, tūtūā and taurekareka as
historically meaningful while examining Western representations of Māori slaves. Its
chronology is useful near the anchor but still weighted toward documentary periods
after 1700.

## The Musket Wars are a transformation, not a baseline

Petrie's synthesis explicitly asks how colonists, trade and war transformed the place
of captives in Māori society.

The much denser nineteenth-century evidence on large-scale captive-taking, exchange and
labor therefore cannot be treated as a transparent snapshot of 1700. It is downstream
evidence for a changing institution.

Required temporal control:
- pre-contact/traditional institution: supported;
- exact 1700 member-community distribution: unresolved;
- early nineteenth-century scale/economics: later transformed context, not back-projected.

## Law / practice

There was no single written Māori legal code or central jurisdiction for the frozen
aggregate.

Status and customary relations are reconstructed from oral histories, early observers,
later ethnography, customary-land evidence and specialist synthesis. This source
condition does not weaken the reality of the institution, but it limits exact-year and
cross-community uniformity claims.

## Network / territory

This is not a territorial-state case.

A captured person could move between communities as a consequence of warfare, but no
Aotearoa-wide “slave trade network” is inferred from the present evidence.

The positive claim concerns social status/practice in traditional Māori communities,
not a uniform territorial practice polygon.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci:
- multiple Māori community traditions synthesized by Petrie;
- pre-contact/traditional social-rank reconstruction in Te Ara;
- early-contact observations and Salmond's 1642–1772 synthesis;
- customary conquest/status relations recorded in later land-tenure evidence.

Maximum defensible inference:

**traditional/pre-contact Māori society included war-captive unfree statuses commonly
described through taurekareka/mōkai and often translated as slavery. This institution is
applicable to the social world surrounding the 1700 anchor, but the evidence does not
establish uniform practice, terminology, prevalence or identical status rules across
all iwi/hapū at exactly 1700.**

Required abstention:
- no single “Māori polity” or Aotearoa-wide legal regime;
- no prevalence estimate;
- no claim that every battle produced captives;
- no automatic taurekareka/mōkai = Atlantic-style chattel slavery mapping;
- no collapse of dependent hapū/tribute relations into slave status;
- no back-projection of Musket Wars scale/economics;
- no practice polygon.

## Source dependency and access audit

- Petrie 2015 is the main specialist synthesis and combines Māori oral sources with
  colonial written records. Later reviews or summaries of the same work are not
  independent attestations.
- Te Ara pages are institutional syntheses and overlap broader ethnographic/historical
  scholarship; their separate pages are not counted as independent event chains.
- Anne Salmond's *Two Worlds* is used indirectly through Te Ara's cited reconstruction;
  the full book was not exhaustively reread in this pass.
- The Waitangi Tribunal report is valuable for category/translation caution but is a
  later legal-historical reconstruction, not an exact 1700 observation.
- Hawkesworth 1773 is a near-anchor observer/editorial textual chain and is used as a
  boundary control, not as proof that Māori generally took no captives.
- Fletcher 2000 systematically examines early documentary sources but is weighted
  toward eighteenth/nineteenth-century records; it supports status/category history,
  not an exact 1700 census.
- Scite remained unavailable because the monthly MCP quota was exhausted. This
  limitation is carried forward; publisher, institutional and repository sources were
  used directly.
- No exhaustive Māori-language oral-history corpus or iwi-by-iwi status reconstruction
  was performed.

## Research disposition

**Bounded-supported, with exact-anchor aggregate distribution unresolved.**

The positive proposition is the existence of war-captive unfreedom/slavery-category
relations in traditional/pre-contact Māori society. The project should not downgrade
that to “inconclusive” merely because exact prevalence at 1700 is unavailable.

The unresolved part is the frozen aggregate's cross-community distribution and exact
status mapping at 1700.

Reopen or deepen if:
- iwi/hapū-specific pre-1769 traditions or source-critical studies can anchor captive
  status to named communities close to 1700;
- specialist work materially revises the pre-contact chronology of taurekareka/mōkai;
- a stronger Māori-language category study changes the current mapping;
- evidence supports or rejects a more specific inter-community captive-transfer
  mechanism.

## Adversarial findings

- **REJECT:** all Māori communities formed one 1700 slavery regime.
- **REJECT:** every war produced captives/slaves.
- **REJECT:** taurekareka or mōkai universally equals Atlantic chattel slavery.
- **REJECT:** dependent hapū paying tribute are automatically slaves.
- **REJECT:** nineteenth-century Musket Wars captive scale can be back-projected.
- **REJECT:** one 1769/1770 “no prisoners” observation disproves the traditional
  captive institution.
- **SUPPORTED:** war-captive unfreedom/slavery-category relations in traditional/
  pre-contact Māori society.
- **UNRESOLVED:** exact 1700 distribution, prevalence and local status rules across
  distinct iwi/hapū.
- **PORTABLE CONTRACT SURVIVES:** period-level positive evidence and aggregate-level
  abstention coexist without converting uncertainty into absence or uniformity.
