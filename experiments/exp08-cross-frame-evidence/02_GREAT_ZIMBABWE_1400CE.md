# Great Zimbabwe — 1400 CE

Target: R1:N:great_zimbabwe_1400. Frozen frame: **node_site**.  
Research stage: **researched_internal**.  
Research outcome: **researched_inconclusive_slavery_with_supported_collective_labor_dependency_and_external_slave_trade_context**.  
EXP-08 packet; no R1 reviewed-state change, canonical update, P-level, geometry
promotion or publication.

## Bounded synthesis

The current evidence does **not** establish or rule out slavery/enslavement at Great
Zimbabwe around the 1400 anchor.

The strongest site-specific political-economy synthesis cuts against a common shortcut:
Great Zimbabwe's monumental walls and hierarchy are not, by themselves, evidence of
forced or enslaved construction labor. Chirikure's synthesis describes production at
the site and in the wider landscape as broadly shared across elite and commoner
households, characterizes decision-making and activity as more collective than coercive,
and interprets drystone construction through communal work parties and other labor.
It also preserves relationships of dependency, reciprocity, land control and rulers'
ability to demand a share of production.

Recent regional synthesis independently supports extraction without resolving slave
status. Delius, Chewins and Forssman describe Great Zimbabwe's 1300–1450 political
economy as drawing tribute from producers in subject homesteads and taxes from traders,
while village-based mining and smelting were not directly controlled by rulers.

There is also real slavery evidence in the connected fourteenth-century Indian Ocean
world. Ibn Battuta's 1331 Kilwa account records slaves at Kilwa and separately reports
the Sofala gold route. Connah's broader archaeological synthesis lists slaves among
commodities received by coastal traders from the African interior, probably moving
through the Save/Sofala corridor. Neither source, however, identifies the enslaved
people as originating at Great Zimbabwe, being held at Great Zimbabwe, or being exported
by Great Zimbabwe's rulers or inhabitants.

The bounded conclusion is therefore:

**Great Zimbabwe at 1400 is researched-inconclusive for slavery/enslavement and for
slave-trade participation. The site does support hierarchy, tribute/extraction,
relationships of dependency and organized collective labor. Slavery in the connected
coastal network is positive context, but current evidence does not support transferring
that status or trade commodity to the Great Zimbabwe node.**

No P-level is assigned.

## Frame and chronology

The frozen target is a **node/site**, not a polygon for every territory politically
linked to Great Zimbabwe.

Specialist synthesis places Great Zimbabwe's apogee within approximately 1300–1450,
so 1400 lies within the relevant political/economic phase. The present research does
not reopen EXP-07's identity qualification.

The maximum spatial inference is the Great Zimbabwe capital/site and claim-specific
connections to its regional trade system. No Great Zimbabwe-state practice polygon is
authorized.

## Monumental construction is not forced-labor evidence

Great Zimbabwe's drystone walls required extensive organization, skill and labor.
Older and popular accounts can easily turn that observation into an unstated forced-
labor premise.

The current site-specific synthesis does not warrant that move. Chirikure finds nearly
uniform production participation across status groups and describes decision-making and
activities as more collective than coercive. Drystone walls are specifically discussed
as products of communal work parties and other labor, within reciprocal relationships
between rulers and ruled.

This is a strong negative control:

- large labor requirement does not establish compelled labor;
- political hierarchy does not establish slave status;
- monumentality does not reveal the juridical/social status of builders;
- reciprocal obligation and dependency may constrain people without being equivalent
  to slavery.

"More collective than coercive" is likewise not proof that coercion or slavery was
absent. Archaeology cannot turn that interpretation into a universal freedom claim.

## Later servants/slaves cannot be back-projected

Chirikure uses historical and ethnographic political economies, especially Mutapa and
Torwa-Changamire, as comparative insight for Great Zimbabwe because the site itself has
limited well-resolved evidence for labor organization.

In that explicitly later **AD 1500–1900** comparison, households could use their own
labor or servants and slaves, as well as reciprocal/cooperative work parties.

That evidence matters as a warning that multiple labor statuses existed in later Shona
political economies. It is not an exact 1400 observation and cannot establish slaves at
Great Zimbabwe merely through cultural or political succession.

## Tribute, dependency and political extraction

Delius, Chewins and Forssman's 2024 synthesis places Great Zimbabwe's heyday at
1300–1450 and describes gold production as village based. Rulers derived wealth through
tribute from producers in subject homesteads and taxation of traders rather than direct
control of mining and smelting.

Chirikure likewise describes ideological and material relationships in which rulers
could demand a share of productive/circulative activity and convert wealth-in-things
into wealth-in-people through dependency and reciprocity.

These are positive political-economy findings. They do not automatically map to:
- slavery;
- debt bondage;
- corvée;
- captive labor;
- hereditary servile status.

The case therefore preserves dependency/extraction without inventing a narrower legal
or labor status.

## Coastal slavery and the provenance boundary

Ibn Battuta's 1331 visit gives unusually close temporal evidence for slavery at Kilwa.
His account also reports gold moving through Sofala. The same source chain therefore
shows that a near-anchor coastal trading center connected to the southern gold trade
contained enslaved people.

This is still not a Great Zimbabwe slave-trade attestation.

The source does not say:
- that Kilwa's slaves came from Great Zimbabwe;
- that Great Zimbabwe rulers supplied captives;
- that gold and enslaved people followed the same inland provenance;
- that enslaved people were present at the Great Zimbabwe capital.

Connah's 2004 archaeological synthesis goes further at the regional level by listing
slaves among commodities coastal traders received from the interior, probably carried
down the Save valley toward Sofala. This is useful directional context, but the statement
does not provide a Great-Zimbabwe-specific captive provenance at the 1400 anchor.

Accordingly:
- **Kilwa slavery:** supported near the anchor;
- **interior-to-Sofala slave movement:** broad secondary context;
- **Great Zimbabwe slave export/holding:** unresolved.

Connected trade is not claim transfer.

## Social hierarchy and archaeological class labels

Great Zimbabwe clearly had political hierarchy and differential status. More recent
archaeological work nevertheless warns that rigid "elite" and "commoner" categories can
be situational and that architectural spaces have been repeatedly reinterpreted.

This matters because neither hierarchy nor spatial separation supplies a slave-status
diagnostic. No household, burial, wall complex or artifact distribution is treated here
as archaeological proof of enslavement merely because it reflects unequal status.

## Category controls

Relevant categories must remain separate:

- communal/cooperative work parties;
- reciprocal labor obligations;
- tribute/tax extraction from subject households;
- political dependency / wealth-in-people;
- servants and slaves in later Mutapa/Torwa comparison;
- enslaved people at Kilwa in 1331;
- broad interior/coastal slave-trade context;
- slavery/enslavement at Great Zimbabwe itself.

Required mappings:
- tribute payer ≠ slave;
- subject household ≠ enslaved household;
- communal builder ≠ forced laborer;
- political dependent ≠ slave by default;
- connected coastal slave market ≠ Great Zimbabwe slave export;
- later Shona slaveholding ≠ exact 1400 Great Zimbabwe evidence.

## Law / practice

No exact-anchor written Great Zimbabwe legal corpus defining slave or servile status
was recovered.

The site must therefore be interpreted through archaeology, political-economy
reconstruction, regional textual evidence and later comparative evidence. The absence
of a written legal corpus is a source condition, not evidence that slavery did not
exist.

## Network / territory

The positive external evidence is directional, not territorial:
- Great Zimbabwe participated in a gold/commodity network reaching the Indian Ocean;
- slavery existed at Kilwa near the anchor;
- broad synthesis places slaves among commodities moving from parts of the African
  interior toward the coast.

These facts do not establish that Great Zimbabwe itself supplied enslaved people.
No state-wide or route-wide slavery classification follows.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci:
- Great Zimbabwe capital/site and surrounding production landscape;
- subject homesteads and regional gold-production networks described in specialist
  synthesis;
- Save/Sofala corridor as broad trade context;
- Kilwa in 1331 as a separate near-anchor slavery locus.

Maximum defensible inference:

**Around 1400, Great Zimbabwe was a hierarchical political/economic center supported by
household production, tribute/extraction, reciprocal/dependency relations and organized
collective labor. The connected Indian Ocean system included slavery, but current
evidence does not establish slavery/enslavement at Great Zimbabwe or Great-Zimbabwe-
specific slave-trade participation.**

Required abstention:
- no forced-labor inference from walls or labor scale;
- no slavery inference from hierarchy, tribute or dependency alone;
- no absence inference from collective/cooperative labor;
- no back-projection of later Mutapa/Torwa servants/slaves;
- no Great Zimbabwe slave-export inference from Kilwa/Sofala connectivity;
- no prevalence estimate;
- no state-wide practice polygon.

## Research disposition

This is **researched-inconclusive**, not an absence finding.

The central missing evidence is claim-specific: a securely dated thirteenth-to-fifteenth
century Great-Zimbabwe-specific attestation or specialist reconstruction of enslaved/
servile status, captive acquisition, slaveholding or slave export.

Additional generic evidence that Great Zimbabwe was hierarchical, built monumental
walls, paid/collected tribute, traded gold, or connected to Kilwa would not change the
permitted conclusion.

Reopen if:
- a specialist source identifies enslaved/servile people specifically at Great Zimbabwe
  in the relevant phase;
- a source-critical study traces enslaved captives through Great Zimbabwe's own
  political/trading institutions near the anchor;
- primary/source-native evidence establishes relevant status, sale, ownership or
  captive transfer;
- new archaeology provides a defensible status interpretation rather than inference
  from inequality alone.

## Adversarial findings

- **REJECT:** huge walls required forced/slave labor.
- **REJECT:** hierarchy or "commoner" status is itself servile/slave status.
- **REJECT:** tribute from subject homesteads establishes slavery.
- **REJECT:** later Mutapa/Torwa slaveholding can be projected backward to 1400.
- **REJECT:** Kilwa slavery plus Sofala/Great Zimbabwe gold connectivity proves Great
  Zimbabwe exported slaves.
- **REJECT:** collective/cooperative labor proves slavery was absent.
- **RESEARCHED-INCONCLUSIVE:** slavery/enslavement at the Great Zimbabwe node at 1400.
- **RESEARCHED-INCONCLUSIVE:** Great-Zimbabwe-specific slave-trade participation.
- **SUPPORTED:** hierarchy, tribute/extraction, dependency/reciprocity and organized
  collective labor.
- **SUPPORTED CONTEXT:** slavery in the connected fourteenth-century coastal network.
- **PORTABLE CONTRACT SURVIVES:** the packet preserves positive dependency/labor
  evidence, connected slavery context and target-level uncertainty without binary
  collapse.
