# Trans-Saharan trade network — 1300 CE

Target: `R1:N:trans_saharan_1300`. Frozen frame: **mobile_network**.  
Research stage: **researched_internal**.  
Research outcome: **BOUNDED_SUPPORTED — movement/trade of enslaved people through bounded parts of the trans-Saharan caravan network is supported for the medieval period spanning the 1300 horizon, with direct mid-fourteenth-century caravan evidence; exact-1300 volume and all-route participation remain unresolved**.  
EXP-14 under issue #290; no R1 reviewed-state change, canonical update, P-level,
geometry promotion, cumulative-candidate integration or publication.

## Bounded synthesis

The strongest evidence package does establish network participation rather than merely
slavery at connected nodes.

Three levels of evidence matter:

1. **medieval specialist synthesis:** Anne Haour explicitly treats a trans-Saharan slave
   trade for approximately AD 750–1500 in which people were sent across the Sahara for
   sale in North Africa and Western Asia;
2. **fourteenth-century economic synthesis:** Sebastian Prange analyzes the
   trans-Saharan slave trade from the fourteenth century onward on routes moving
   enslaved people from Sudanic Africa through Sahelian and Saharan entrepôts to the
   Maghrib;
3. **bounded primary-route evidence:** Ibn Battuta's 1353 return from the central Sudan
   toward Morocco was made with a large caravan carrying **600 female slaves**. Modern
   specialist syntheses trace the report to the *Rihla* / Levtzion-Hopkins source
   tradition and place the caravan on the Takedda–Tuat–Sijilmasa northbound corridor.

Together these support the proposition:

> **The medieval trans-Saharan network around the 1300 horizon included actual
> northbound movement of enslaved people, not merely slavery in connected societies.
> Direct route evidence is strongest in the mid-fourteenth century and specialist
> synthesis places that traffic within a longer medieval system spanning the anchor.**

This does **not** establish:
- an exact number moved in 1300;
- uniform slave traffic on every Saharan route;
- a fixed network polygon;
- slavery in every city/node;
- commercial sale as the fate of every enslaved person transported;
- later fifteenth–nineteenth-century scale at the 1300 horizon.

No P-level is assigned.

## Temporal interpretation

The target anchor is 1300 CE.

The evidence has two different temporal roles:

- Haour's specialist synthesis explicitly covers approximately AD 750–1500, placing
  trans-Saharan slave trading across the selected anchor as a medieval phenomenon;
- Prange treats the organized economic system from the fourteenth century onward;
- the clearest bounded route event is Ibn Battuta's 1353 northbound caravan.

The project therefore records:
**medieval system spanning the anchor + direct near-anchor fourteenth-century event**.

It does not invent an exact-year 1300 caravan or quantify 1300 traffic.

## Direct route movement — Ibn Battuta's 1353 caravan

John Wright's specialist history of the trans-Saharan slave trade and Chouki El Hamel's
history of slavery in Morocco both use Ibn Battuta's return journey as direct evidence.

After travelling in Mali/Gao and reaching Takedda, Ibn Battuta joined a northbound
caravan in September 1353. The caravan included **600 female slaves** and crossed the
Sahara toward Morocco through the Tuat/Sijilmasa corridor.

This is the most important discriminator for EXP-14 because it shows:
- enslaved people physically moving in a trans-Saharan caravan;
- a Sudanic/Saharan-to-Maghrib direction;
- movement embedded in a commercial caravan system rather than inferred only from
  court possession or a market at one endpoint.

What it does **not** prove:
- that 600 was an annual average;
- that the women all shared the same origin/status history;
- that every western or central trans-Saharan route carried comparable numbers;
- that the same volume existed in 1300.

El Hamel discusses the possibility of regular annual traffic, but any extrapolation of
600 into an annual average is explicitly **not adopted** here.

## Taghaza — node-level coerced labour, not network-volume evidence

On his southbound journey in 1352, Ibn Battuta described the Taghaza salt settlement as
inhabited by slaves of the Massufa who mined salt.

This is useful evidence that coerced/slave labour existed at a Saharan network node.

It does not by itself establish:
- where those enslaved workers originated;
- whether they had been transported through the same long-distance commercial flow;
- network-wide slave-trade volume.

The Taghaza passage is therefore retained as **node-level practice context** and is not
double-counted as a second independent trans-Saharan transport event.

## Specialist medieval continuity

Anne Haour's archaeological/historical chapter is a key interpretive control because it
defines the relevant medieval phenomenon broadly enough to include the anchor while
remaining cautious about archaeological visibility.

Her period is approximately AD 750–1500 and the object is specifically movement of
enslaved persons **across the Sahara** toward North Africa and Western Asia.

This supports temporal continuity at the level of the trade system.

It does not provide a quantified annual series or justify a route-wide prevalence map.

## Economic organization in the fourteenth century

Sebastian Prange's study begins with the fourteenth century and focuses on routes that
moved slaves from Sudanic Africa through Sahelian/Saharan entrepôts to the Maghrib.

Its relevance is structural:
- slave traffic used genuine caravan/commercial organization;
- cooperation and intermediary institutions mattered;
- the network cannot be reduced to one polity or city.

The paper extends much later than the target period.

Only its fourteenth-century framing is used here; later organization/scale is not
projected backward.

## Mansa Musa 1324 is not used as the decisive trade proof

Popular and museum accounts often describe Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage as including
large numbers of enslaved attendants.

The numbers and terminology vary substantially across later retellings, and the
earliest al-Umari tradition is not a clean commercial-slave-caravan record.

EXP-14 therefore does **not** depend on the famous 12,000-slaves version.

At most, the pilgrimage is context for movement of a very large Mali entourage through
the same Saharan connectivity. It is not counted as an independent commercial
slave-trade attestation.

## Network vs territorial practice

This target is a **mobile_network**.

The evidence supports:
- slave-trade participation in the medieval trans-Saharan system;
- specific northbound movement on at least one central/western corridor;
- slave labour at at least one Saharan node.

The evidence does not support:
- territorial slavery in every network node;
- one legal regime;
- one political owner;
- one fixed route;
- a Sahara-wide historical practice polygon.

Mali, Takedda, Taghaza, Tuat, Sijilmasa and Maghrebi destinations remain distinct
loci/roles.

## Volume / prevalence control

No volume estimate is produced.

The 600-person Ibn Battuta caravan is a bounded event.

It is invalid to infer:
- 600 per year;
- 600 per caravan;
- 600 per route;
- network-wide annual totals;
- demographic prevalence.

Later quantitative estimates for the early-modern/modern trans-Saharan trade are not
back-projected.

## Source dependency and access audit

- Haour 2011 is the principal specialist medieval-period synthesis.
- Prange 2006 is an independent economic-organization synthesis, but extends far beyond
  the anchor; only its fourteenth-century scope is used.
- Wright 2007 is a specialist synthetic history and is the main secondary bridge to the
  medieval caravan evidence.
- Ibn Battuta's 1352–1353 evidence is one primary textual family, regardless of modern
  editions/translations.
- El Hamel 2013 independently discusses the 1353 caravan in a Moroccan slavery history
  but cites the same Ibn Battuta / Levtzion-Hopkins source chain; it is not counted as
  an independent primary attestation.
- Guérin 2017 supports the strength of thirteenth-century trans-Saharan connectivity
  under Mali, but does not independently prove slave movement and is used as network
  context only.
- No exhaustive Arabic manuscript comparison or route-by-route medieval archive search
  is claimed.

## Research disposition

**BOUNDED_SUPPORTED.**

Supported:
- a medieval trans-Saharan slave trade spanning the selected 1300 horizon in specialist
  scholarship;
- direct movement of 600 female slaves in a northbound trans-Saharan caravan in 1353;
- fourteenth-century commercial organization moving enslaved people from Sudanic
  Africa through Saharan/Sahelian entrepôts toward the Maghrib;
- bounded slave labour at Taghaza as node-level context.

Not supported:
- an exact 1300 caravan count;
- network-wide annual volume;
- every route participating equally;
- territorial slavery in every connected polity/node;
- every captive entering commerce;
- Mansa Musa pilgrimage retinue numbers as decisive commercial evidence;
- a fixed route/practice polygon.
