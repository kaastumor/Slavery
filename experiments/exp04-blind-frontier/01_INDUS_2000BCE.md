## Bounded synthesis so far

A positive slavery interpretation exists in the literature and must be retained.
The presently recovered materials do not yet justify an atlas designation of
civilization-wide territorial practice at 2000 BCE. This is an unfinished review,
not a completed inconclusive result, an absence claim, or an automatic dispute label.

Kenoyer 2011, Table 1 (printed p. 4), places the Harappan phase at 2600–1900 BCE;
Table 2 (p. 5) places Harappa period 3C around 2200–1900 BCE. These support an
approximate archaeological-period frame, not exact-year slavery applicability.
His discussion of seals (pp. 9–10) interprets elite power, unlike Green's reconstruction.

Habib 2002, pp. 49 and 58, reports enslaved people among Meluhha's exports and
uses that alongside domestic architecture to argue for substantial slavery.
Preserve both the positive interpretation and its evidentiary route. His domestic
passage (p. 40) also allows joint-family accommodation as an alternative to household
servants/slaves. Network export and house layout cannot silently become a uniform
territorial institution or a prevalence estimate in the atlas.

Green 2020/2021 challenges assumptions that Indus urban complexity required a
coercive ruling class. His discussion of Harappa's Mound F questions the granary
identification. This limits an architectural inference; it does not establish the
absence of slavery or directly refute every reported Meluhha transaction.

Rao 2018 proposes a rationing interpretation for Harappan tablets by comparison
with Near Eastern accounting (pp. 518–521). It is a hypothesis, not a deciphered
slave-status record. The publication reproduces archaeological objects, but the
packet has not independently inspected their excavation catalogue/context.

## P453801 / Laursen–Steinkeller reconciliation

The bounded primary record is **CDLI P453801 / Nisaba 15 371**. The pinned CDLI
revision 2249158 (2015-09-15) identifies Nanaza, Samar and Samar's wife Ali-ahi as
people of Meluhha receiving oil rations. On the reverse it reads a-ru-a lugal,
translated in that CDLI revision as “Royal offerings”, followed by their work as
herdsmen of mountain goats. Current CDLI search metadata places the administrative
tablet at Irisagrig (modern location uncertain), in the Ur III period, and dates it
Šū-Suen year 6, month 12. This is a Mesopotamian record and a regnal date; no exact
absolute BCE year is imposed here.

A Harappa.com page reproduces a passage it attributes to Laursen and Steinkeller
2017, pp. 83–84. In that specialist interpretation, Nanaza and Samar are treated as
royal donated slaves and probably as people from Meluhha or a neighboring region.
The same discussion explicitly says their route into Babylonia cannot be known, then
offers the narrower hypothesis that the crown acquired them somewhere in the Gulf,
possibly through Ur III commercial activity. That hypothesis is not acquisition
provenance and does not locate their enslavement in the Indus region.

This resolves an important apparent conflict: CDLI's English rendering and
Laursen–Steinkeller's social-status interpretation are two layers on the **same
artifact**, not independent attestations. a-ru-a lugal cannot be mapped to a modern
slavery category merely from CDLI's lexical rendering. Conversely, the
Laursen–Steinkeller interpretation must be preserved rather than flattened back to
“offering”.

Specialist work on a-ru-a also cautions against universal equivalence with “slave”.
Vitali Bartash's author-posted abstract on Ur III dedicatees describes a-ru-a
people as a strongly dependent group whose origins could include foreign prisoners,
slaves, or family members, while arguing that their status differed from both slaves
and free citizens. This contextual source does not overturn Laursen–Steinkeller's
text-specific reading; it makes the category mapping explicitly source-specific.

The current CDLI reading na-na-sa3 differs from the older revision's na-na-za.
Preserve both versions rather than silently replacing the older spelling or inferring
identity from either name. The tablet's Mesopotamian provenance, a Meluhha
designation, and an external servile/dependent interpretation still do not establish
an Indus enslavement event, a territorial institution, recurrence, or prevalence.
Neither the record nor the regnal date establishes exact 2000 BCE applicability.

they are distinct interpretations, not three independent positive attestations.

P453801, the Harappa reproduction and Laursen–Steinkeller's discussion are one
citation/evidence chain for the same Mesopotamian tablet. Bartash is a separate
terminological/status synthesis, not a second attestation of Nanaza or Samar.

## Adversarial findings

- **REVISE:** treating CDLI “Royal offerings” versus specialist “royal donated slaves”
  as a contradiction would double-count one artifact and confuse lexical translation
  with social-status interpretation.
- **REVISE:** Laursen–Steinkeller's “somewhere in the Gulf” acquisition suggestion
  cannot be promoted to an Indus enslavement location; the authors explicitly retain
  uncertainty about how the individuals reached Babylonia.
- **REVISE:** a-ru-a cannot be globally normalized to “slave”; preserve the
  text-specific specialist interpretation and broader category uncertainty together.
- **SURVIVES, provisional:** D-089 fields can retain source version, dependency,
  term/category mapping, external-versus-territorial scope and an access blocker while
  keeping the target under review. This is not a completed portability verdict.
- **REJECT:** origin, export, Meluhha designation, or geometry automatically proves
  local enslavement, institutional slavery, scale, nationality, or exact 2000 BCE state.
