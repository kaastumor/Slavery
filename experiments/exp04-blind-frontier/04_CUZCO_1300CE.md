# Cuzco — 1300 CE

Target: R1:L:1300:B. Frame: **node_or_incipient_polity**, not the later imperial
capital projected backward.
Research stage: **under_review**. Research outcome: **unassessed**.
Experimental packet, 2026-09-24; no independent historical review, canonical update,
P-level assignment, geometry promotion or publication.

## Bounded synthesis

Cuzco at 1300 CE sits inside the Killke / Late Intermediate Period and before the
well-documented imperial transformation of the Inca state. Archaeological syntheses
place the major transition between AD 1000 and 1400 and the first expansion outside
the Cuzco region only in the late 1300s or early 1400s. The frozen target therefore
must not inherit the mature Tawantinsuyu's institutions by default.

Later Inca evidence clearly documents strong forms of labor dependency, including
yanacona/yana, mitimaes and mita obligations. But the category historically most often
compared to slavery, yanacona, is itself disputed: recent specialist synthesis treats
it as a heterogeneous form of strong asymmetric dependency rather than a simple slave
status. The same synthesis states that yana practices had pre-Inca roots, yet all
written sources for yanacona are colonial-era sources and do not establish a
Cuzco-specific 1300 CE attestation.

The present evidence therefore supports a **plausible pre-Inca dependency background**
but not a territorial slavery/coerced-labor classification for the frozen Cuzco anchor.
This is not absence. It is an anchor/source problem combined with a category-mapping
problem.

No P-level is assigned.

## Identity and temporal frame

Bauer and Covey place the Cuzco region's Killke-period state-development process
between AD 1000 and 1400. Their synthesis describes an evolution from multiple
competing complex polities toward a more integrated state, with expansion beyond the
Cuzco region beginning only in the late 1300s or early 1400s.

Covey's longer archaeological synthesis likewise emphasizes that the centuries
AD 1000–1400 had little coinciding documentary evidence and must be reconstructed by
combining archaeology with a critical rereading of later Spanish chronicles.

Accordingly:
- Cuzco at 1300 is an **incipient/local polity or node**, not Tawantinsuyu;
- later imperial institutions may supply comparison or possible lineage, not automatic
  continuity;
- mature royal estates, imperial resettlement and provincial tribute systems cannot be
  backdated to the anchor without independent evidence.

## Yana / yanacona: strong dependency, disputed slavery mapping

Noack and Nowack's 2023 specialist synthesis makes the category boundary explicit.

They define yanacona broadly as people detached from their family group, freed from
ordinary tribute and serving Inca or provincial elites, while emphasizing overlap with
other categories such as mitimaes and camayoc. They state that the degree and lived
form of dependency varied substantially.

Most importantly for this target:
- all written sources about yanacona are from the Spanish colonial period;
- the authors argue that yana practices had pre-Inca roots in local social
  organization;
- that pre-Inca statement is a specialist reconstruction from retrospective evidence,
  not a contemporary Cuzco-1300 record;
- war captivity was one route into yana status during Inca expansion, but other routes
  existed;
- the chapter's conclusion rejects a simple equation of yanacona with slaves and
  instead describes strong asymmetric dependency with heterogeneous status and
  privilege.

The correct Atlas treatment is therefore not “yana = slave”. It is a historically
specific dependency category whose mapping depends on mechanism, rights, obligations,
heritability, mobility, elite relation and source context.

## Imperial-period archaeology cannot close the 1300 question

Hu and Quave's 2020 archaeological study demonstrates that imperial Inka
mitmaqkuna and yanakuna could be unfree/resettled labor populations and that their
lived material conditions cannot be inferred from elite narratives of privilege.
Their Cuzco-heartland case includes Cheqoq.

That is valuable category and method evidence, but it belongs to the mature imperial
system. It cannot be used as a source-native attestation that the same institution
operated in Cuzco at 1300.

Similarly, later Cuzco evidence for royal estates, yanacona serving deceased rulers'
lineages, state redistribution and large-scale mita labor is chronologically downstream
of the frozen target.

## Archaeology around the anchor

The strongest archaeological literature for c. 1300 is about state formation,
settlement, political competition, ceramics, fortification, agricultural
intensification and regional hierarchy. It can support an incipient-polity frame and
social differentiation.

It does not, by itself, identify a particular laborer as enslaved or yana. Material
inequality, warfare, fortification or public works are not sufficient category
evidence.

This prevents a common shortcut: using evidence that early Cuzco could mobilize labor
or sustain hierarchy as proof of slavery or a particular coerced-labor institution.

## Recovery / reopening condition

Reopen the Cuzco classification when target-specific evidence securely tied to
roughly AD 1200–1350 can establish one or more of the following:
1. an early Cuzco/Killke labor-status category with explicit rights/obligations;
2. a securely dated captive-to-dependent pathway in the Cuzco polity;
3. archaeological households/estates demonstrably occupied by permanent dependent
   laborers before imperial expansion;
4. a specialist source-critical reconstruction that explicitly anchors yana or an
   equivalent institution in early Cuzco rather than generic pre-Inca Andes;
5. source-native terminology whose colonial transmission and chronological scope can
   be bounded.

Useful recovery sources include the full Bauer/Covey state-development chapter,
Covey's open-access heartland monograph, source-critical studies of early Cuzco oral/
colonial traditions, and any securely dated Killke household/labor archaeology.

Do not treat absence of writing at 1300 as historical absence.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci:
- Cuzco Valley / Killke archaeology for the anchor frame;
- later imperial Cuzco and Cheqoq for comparison only;
- wider pre-Inca Andes for possible institutional lineage only;
- colonial written records as retrospective evidence requiring source criticism.

Maximum current inference:
**Cuzco around 1300 was an emerging local polity within the Killke-period
transformation, and later scholarship reconstructs pre-Inca roots for yana-style
dependency, but current evidence does not securely anchor that institution or slavery
to Cuzco at 1300.**

Required abstention:
- do not project mature Tawantinsuyu into 1300 Cuzco;
- do not equate yana/yanacona with slave;
- do not treat mita, mitimaes, camayoc and yanacona as one coercion class;
- do not infer slave status from hierarchy, warfare, public works or resettlement
  without category-specific evidence;
- do not treat colonial chroniclers as contemporary 1300 witnesses;
- do not infer prevalence from later imperial scale;
- do not use later imperial Cuzco geometry as the 1300 polity boundary.

## Law / practice and network / territory

No contemporary Cuzco-1300 legal text was recovered that defines a slavery or yana
status. Later reported Inca rules and colonial administrative descriptions are
retrospective and belong to different political conditions.

Later imperial relocation networks likewise cannot prove territorial practice at the
anchor. Origin, resettlement and labor status remain separate claims.

## Adversarial findings

- **REJECT:** imperial Inca dependency systems can simply be back-projected to Cuzco
  in 1300.
- **REJECT:** because yanacona were strongly dependent, they should be normalized as
  slaves. Current specialist synthesis explicitly disputes that equivalence.
- **REVISE:** “pre-Inca origins” is direct evidence for Cuzco 1300. It is a broad
  specialist reconstruction from later sources and needs a target-specific anchor.
- **REJECT:** Killke hierarchy, warfare or labor mobilization proves slavery.
- **SURVIVES, provisional:** D-089 can preserve the incipient-polity frame,
  retrospective-source limitation, category dispute and chronology boundary while
  leaving the target under review.
- **PARK:** territorial slavery/coerced-labor classification until target-specific
  early Cuzco evidence is recovered.
