# Andaman Islands communities — 1800 CE

Experimental packet, 2026-09-24; no independent historical review, canonical update,

## Bounded synthesis

At the 1800 CE anchor, two different questions must be kept separate.

First, coercion directed **at Andamanese people by British colonial actors** is
positively documented around the anchor. The East India Company established its first
Andaman settlement in 1789 and abandoned it in the 1790s. Specialist historical work
shows that British exploration and colonization in the late eighteenth and nineteenth
centuries used kidnap and confinement of indigenous islanders as a deliberate contact
and knowledge-making practice. A historical compilation specifically records
Andamanese being taken to Calcutta in 1790.

Second, this does **not** establish slavery or coerced-labor institutions **within
indigenous Andamanese communities themselves**. Later ethnography reconstructing
precolonial Great Andaman social organization describes small autonomous local groups
and families, but its silence on slavery cannot be converted into an absence claim,
especially because it was written after catastrophic colonial demographic and social
change.

The correct result is therefore multidimensional:
- **external/colonial coercion and captivity near the anchor: supported**;
- **indigenous territorial/internal slavery or servile dependency: unassessed**;
- **no region-wide prevalence or uniform-community claim**.

No P-level is assigned.

## Identity and frame

“Andaman Islands communities” is not one people or polity.

Radcliffe-Brown's retrospective ethnography distinguishes multiple tribes/language
groups and emphasizes that local groups were independent and autonomous. The “tribe”
was a loose aggregate with limited role in regulating social life.

Accordingly:
- evidence about one Great Andaman group does not automatically apply to Jarawa,
  Onge, Sentinelese or every other island community;
- colonial “Andamanese” labels can flatten distinct groups and must be treated as
  source categories rather than modern proof of identity;
- a colonial settlement's jurisdiction cannot be substituted for indigenous social
  territory.

## Anchor-period colonial captivity

### Company settlement, 1789–1796

The East India Company established a settlement in the Andamans in 1789. This
colonial presence overlaps the frozen 1800 anchor closely enough to establish an
immediate predecessor context, but the settlement had already been abandoned by
1796. The packet therefore uses **near-anchor** rather than exact-cross-section
language.

Clare Anderson's specialist study of 1771–1864 argues that indigenous captivity was
central to British attempts at colonization. British explorers and officials captured
islanders to observe them, learn languages and use them as hoped-for intermediaries.
Her evidence covers the late eighteenth-century experimental phase as well as the later
penal-colony period.

A compiled contact chronology records that in 1790 Andamanese were taken to Fort
William/Calcutta to be exhibited. This is a bounded removal/captivity event close to
the anchor. The exact community identity is not treated as certain merely from the
later compilation's probability statement.

This evidence supports **colonial external coercion**, not indigenous slavery.

## Later colonial institutionalization: continuity, not anchor substitution

The permanent penal settlement began only in 1858. Anderson shows that later
“Andaman Home” policies institutionalized earlier capture practices: islanders could be
confined, restrained and forced into forms of productive dependency.

This later material is important because it demonstrates continuity and escalation in
colonial coercive practice. It is not backdated to 1800 as though the same institutions
already existed then.

Likewise, the transportation of tens of thousands of Indian/Burmese convicts to the
Andamans after 1858 is a major coerced-labor history of the islands but lies outside
the frozen anchor and concerns a different population/status system.

## Indigenous social organization: what the evidence can and cannot say

Radcliffe-Brown's 1922 study explicitly attempts to reconstruct Great Andaman social
organization as it existed before European occupation. It describes:
- independent autonomous local groups;
- loose language-based tribal aggregates;
- families as the principal internal subdivision;
- local-group land rights and private ownership of some property;
- elders/social sanctions rather than a centralized coercive political apparatus.

This is useful contextual evidence but is methodologically limited:
- the reconstruction was produced long after initial contact;
- indigenous populations had undergone severe demographic and social disruption;
- the author explicitly acknowledges points for which satisfactory information could
  not be obtained;
- the study primarily concerns Great Andaman and cannot be generalized to every
  island community.

Most importantly, **failure to mention slavery is not evidence of absence**. The
ethnography was not a complete slave-status census and its source situation was deeply
post-contact.

## Category mapping

Relevant categories must remain separate:
- **kidnap/capture/confinement** by British actors;
- **convict transportation/penal labor**, especially after 1858;
- colonial attempts to create dependent indigenous intermediaries;
- indigenous adoption, marriage, feud/capture or other social relationships;
- slavery/chattel property, which requires its own evidence.

Kidnapping an Andamanese person is a severe coercive act, but it is not automatically
the same institution as chattel slavery. Conversely, refusing the “slavery” label does
not erase the coercion.

This case therefore tests the Atlas's ability to preserve morally and historically
significant coercion without forcing every form into one universal status category.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci:
- Company exploration/settlement interactions in the Andamans in the late eighteenth
  century;
- the 1790 removal to Calcutta as external captivity;
- Great Andaman retrospective ethnography for indigenous social context;
- later Port Blair/Andaman Home system as downstream colonial continuity.

Maximum defensible inference:
**British colonial actors used capture/removal of Andamanese people during the
late-eighteenth-century contact/settlement period close to the 1800 anchor; current
evidence does not establish an indigenous region-wide slavery or servile-labor
institution at that anchor.**

Required abstention:
- no inference that indigenous Andamanese practiced slavery from colonial captivity;
- no inference that they did not practice slavery from ethnographic silence;
- no uniform claim across distinct Andaman communities;
- no backdating of the 1858 penal colony or 1863 Andaman Home;
- no conflation of convict labor, indigenous captivity and slavery;
- no prevalence estimate;
- no modern archipelago polygon as a social or political jurisdiction.

## Law / practice and external / territorial separation

The near-anchor positive evidence concerns **external practice by colonial actors**.
No indigenous written legal regime exists for comparison, and colonial law/jurisdiction
cannot be treated as indigenous social law.

The target therefore demonstrates why “territorial practice” needs an actor/locus
qualification: coercion occurring **in/around the Andamans** can be historically real
without becoming evidence that **Andamanese communities themselves** maintained that
institution.

## Geometry state

**fuzzy_region_or_unresolved.**

The archipelago is geographically identifiable, but community territories and contact
zones varied. A modern Andaman administrative polygon must not become one indigenous
political or practice polygon. External removal to Calcutta also requires an
event/network representation rather than territorial recoloring alone.

## Dependency and access audit

- Anderson 2011 is the main specialist synthesis for the colonial kidnap/confinement
  claim; institutional pages repeating its abstract are not independent evidence.
- The 1790 contact chronology derives from historical records/secondary compilation
  and needs source-native archival verification before becoming a canonical event.
- Radcliffe-Brown 1922 is a historical ethnography based partly on later fieldwork and
  indigenous recollection after colonial disruption; it is not a contemporary 1800
  census.
- Later penal-colony scholarship overlaps Anderson's archival corpus and is used for
  period separation rather than independent anchor corroboration.

English-language historical scholarship and digitized historical ethnography were
reviewed. No indigenous-language oral-history corpus or original India Office file for
the 1790 removal was independently read in this pass.

- **REJECT:** Radcliffe-Brown's reconstructed social organization proves absence of
  slavery.
- **REVISE:** later penal labor/Andaman Home evidence can be shown as if it existed
  in 1800. Keep it downstream.
- **REJECT:** “Andamanese” is one homogeneous society.
