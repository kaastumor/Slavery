# Sumerian City-States — 2000 BCE

Research stage: **researched_internal**. Research outcome:
**bounded_supported_with_transition_and_category_boundaries**.
Experimental packet, 2026-09-24; no independent historical review, canonical update,
P-level assignment, geometry promotion or publication.

## Bounded synthesis

The frozen target label must not be read as a single “Sumerian state.” Around the
2000 BCE anchor, southern Mesopotamia crosses the terminal Ur III / early Isin-Larsa
transition. In the conventional middle chronology used by Adams, Ibbi-Suen's reign
ends in 2004 BCE; modern periodization likewise places the collapse of the Ur III
state around 2004 BCE and the rise of local dynasties immediately afterward. The
target therefore represents an aggregate of changing city-state contexts across a
political transition, not a stable territorial jurisdiction.

Within that limit, the historical evidence for slavery immediately before and around
the transition is strong. Late Ur III scholarship and edited records document people
who could be bought and sold, inherited, freed, enslaved through debt or punishment,
or taken as captives. The evidence includes private household ownership, court
disputes about slave status and sale, individual purchase records from Ur and Umma,
and large household/institutional groups at Garshana and Irisagrig. This supports a
bounded conclusion that slavery was a legally and socially recognized institution in
multiple southern Mesopotamian contexts near the anchor.

It does **not** support treating every dependent worker as enslaved, assigning one
condition to all city-states, quantifying prevalence from the unusually dense Ur III
archives, or converting late-Ur III evidence into an exact 2000 BCE territorial state.
Farber's 2021 study of prices and wages across Babylonia ca. 2000–1600 BCE includes
slave prices among its source categories and supports continuity of slave transactions
into the succeeding Isin-Larsa/Old Babylonian evidence base, but it is broad-period
evidence rather than a single exact-year attestation.

No P-level is assigned in EXP-04. The evidence is compatible with an
institutional/systemic dimension of slavery near the transition, but any later canonical
P-level would still need target-frame and temporal review rather than being derived
from text counts, transaction counts or archive density.

## Identity and temporal frame

The frozen registry already marks this target as a **polity aggregate** and warns
against treating it as one sovereign territorial polity. That limitation is material.

Adams 2010 frames his Garshana synthesis around Šu-Suen (2037–2029 BCE in his
conventional middle chronology) and Ibbi-Suen, whose reign ends in 2004 BCE. The
Metropolitan Museum's period overview likewise places the Ur III collapse at ca.
2004 BCE and describes the following era as one in which rulers controlled individual
cities and local dynasties formed.

Accordingly:
- late Ur III evidence can establish a strong **near-anchor predecessor** state;
- early Isin-Larsa evidence can establish broader **post-transition continuity**;
- neither may silently be relabeled as an exact observation at 2000 BCE;
- regnal dates are retained in source-native form where available.

## Source-native and specialist evidence

### Ur: UET 3, 0026 / CDLI P136342

CDLI records this Sumerian tablet and envelope as excavated at Ur and dated
Šū-Suen year 1. The text lists one `sag nita₂`, names the person Nanna-saga,
gives a price of two shekels of silver and uses a purchase formula. ePSD2's Ur III
lexical entry maps `saŋnita` / `sag-nita₂` to “male slave” in its Ur III
corpus. This is a bounded source-native sale attestation at Ur; the lexical resource
supports the status reading but is not a second historical event.

### Umma: Molina 2008, BM 106550

Molina's specialist edition of Ur III court records gives BM 106550, dated
Amar-Suen year 2, intercalary month 12. In the case, Geme-Ašar bought Ur-Bau for
five shekels of silver; the woman escaped before completing the established period
and the sellers were required to repay the purchase price. The record closes as a case
concerning citizens of Umma. This independently shows sale, ownership-related
obligation, flight and adjudication in an Umma legal context.

CDLI also preserves AnOr 7, 321 / P101616 from Umma, dated Amar-Suen year 2,
whose edited translation identifies Šu-Erra as a slave bought for ten shekels of silver.
It is another artifact, but the packet does not convert multiple sale texts from the
same documentary tradition into a prevalence measure.

### Irisagrig: Nisaba 15, 0953 / CDLI P454138

CDLI metadata identifies P454138 as an Ur III tablet from Irisagrig dated
Ibbi-Suen year 3. Bertrand Lafont's 2013 specialist discussion of the newly published
Irisagrig archive interprets Nisaba 15, 953 as a twelve-column inventory of Ninsaga's
household, totaling 225 adults and children and characterizing the relevant
`geme₂-arad₂` dependents as slaves. This is useful evidence for a large household
group late in the dynasty.

The CDLI artifact and Lafont's interpretation are one evidence chain, not two
independent attestations. The original REFEMA page was blocked by anti-bot access in
this run; an indexed reproduction of the article was read and the CDLI publication
metadata/artifact record were checked separately. The claim therefore retains that
access/dependency qualification.

### Garshana: slavery and mixed labour

Adams 2010 describes Garshana as employing many hundreds of slaves and hired
workers in the late Ur III period. Crucially, he also reports that free laborers
considerably outnumbered slaves in the surviving daily totals and that `geme₂`
could designate a working woman whether slave or free, with status recoverable only
from context. Garshana therefore supports both institutional presence and the strongest
warning against converting a labor-list label into slave status.

### Private households and court practice

Neumann 2011 emphasizes the uneven archival geography of Ur III documentation and
argues that slavery did not constitute the dominant mode of production or the main
class of workers. At the same time, he documents private household slavery, sale
contracts, inheritance holdings, debt enslavement and court disputes over slave status.
He notes that actual numbers in private ownership are difficult to judge. This
simultaneously strengthens the institutional-presence claim and limits any prevalence
claim.

## Law is not practice

The Laws of Ur-Namma composite contains explicit provisions concerning male and
female slaves, manumission, marriage/status consequences and sale/ownership disputes.
It is evidence that slave status was legible in a legal-normative tradition.

It is **not** used as proof that every provision was enforced uniformly near 2000 BCE,
nor as a substitute for practice. The sale records, court cases, household inventories
and workplace material provide separate practice evidence. The law composite is
earlier than the anchor and is retained as legal/status context, not exact-year evidence.

## Category mapping: do not collapse dependency into slavery

The Sumer case is a strong positive slavery case precisely because several adjacent
categories must remain separate.

- **`geme₂`** can mean an enslaved woman in some contexts, but Adams shows that at
  Garshana it could also denote a working woman who was free. Context, not the token
  alone, decides the mapping.
- **`saŋnita` / `sag-nita₂`** is mapped by ePSD2 to male slave in the Ur III
  corpus and is supported in P136342 by a purchase context. This does not authorize
  universal cross-period normalization of every `sag` compound.
- **`un-il₂`** is analyzed by Pottorf 2025 as a serflike Ur III status group:
  legally free, subject to full-time mandatory work, and socially/economically
  distinct from both citizens and slaves. It must not be counted as slavery merely
  because coercion/dependency was substantial.
- **`a-ru-a`** / dedicated personnel cannot be normalized to “temple slave.”
  Bartash 2025 shows heterogeneous origins including captives and former slaves but
  argues that the dedicated category requires a more specific dependency analysis.
- **captives / `nam-ra-ak`** can be a source of enslaved people, but captivity,
  origin and subsequent legal/social status remain distinct claims.
- **hired workers** at Garshana are explicitly distinct from slaves even when they
  appear in the same labor system.

The Indus packet's P453801 / `a-ru-a` chain is therefore **not reused as an
independent Sumer attestation**. It is only a cross-case reminder of the same
category-mapping hazard. Bartash 2025 supplies the broader specialist category
analysis used here.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci recovered in this pass include Ur, Umma, Garshana in the Umma
provincial context, Nippur/private legal traditions, and Irisagrig, plus broader
southern-Babylonian specialist synthesis.

Maximum defensible inference:
**slavery was an institution documented in multiple southern Mesopotamian urban,
household, legal and labor contexts immediately before the c. 2000 BCE political
transition, with broader evidence for continued slave transactions in the succeeding
Isin-Larsa/Old Babylonian evidence base.**

Required abstention:
- no single sovereignty or uniform territorial regime for “Sumerian City-States”;
- no exact 2000 BCE observation manufactured from a 2004 BCE dynastic boundary;
- no prevalence or labor-share estimate from document density;
- no claim that slavery was the dominant labor mode;
- no automatic slave mapping for `geme₂`, `un-il₂`, `a-ru-a`, captives or
  generic dependents;
- no ethnicity/nationality inference from personal names;
- no geometry-derived strengthening of historical evidence.

## Network / territorial separation

War captives and movement of dependent people matter to the history of slavery, but
this packet does not need a network claim to establish bounded local practice. A
person's foreign origin, a campaign, or a transfer between households does not prove
a uniform practice at the origin place or across every polity in the aggregate.

The target's territorial dimension therefore remains **multi-locus / aggregate**,
not a single political polygon.

## Search trail and useful exclusions

Source families inspected: CDLI artifacts/composites and bibliography; ePSD2;
Ur III specialist slavery/household scholarship; Garshana/Irisagrig studies; recent
dependency-status scholarship; Isin-Larsa chronology/economic synthesis.

Useful exclusions:
- Old Babylonian **model contracts** are school exercises, not functional legal
  documents, so they were not used as direct practice attestations.
- literary uses of “slave” in royal letters/hymns were not used as socioeconomic
  presence evidence where rhetorical usage could suffice;
- later Larsa material can demonstrate longer continuity but cannot be backdated to
  the 2000 BCE anchor;
- the very large number of surviving Ur III texts is an archive fact, not a prevalence
  estimator.

## Adversarial findings

- **REVISE:** treating “Sumerian City-States — 2000 BCE” as one polity would create a
  false territorial object. Preserve the aggregate frame and transition explicitly.
- **REJECT:** `geme₂`, ration receipt, labor obligation or dependence by itself
  proves slavery. Garshana directly supplies a counterexample.
- **REJECT:** `un-il₂` should be folded into slavery because work was compulsory.
  Pottorf's specialist analysis treats it as legally free and serflike, intermediate
  between citizens and slaves.
- **REVISE:** `a-ru-a` should be normalized as “temple slave.” Bartash's analysis
  requires a more specific dedicated/dependent category.
- **REVISE:** strong late-Ur III evidence can be labeled “exactly 2000 BCE.”
  The correct temporal statement is near-anchor/transition, with succeeding-period
  continuity kept separate.
- **REJECT:** numerous tablets or sales imply that slavery dominated labor or society.
  Neumann and Adams explicitly constrain that inference.
- **SURVIVES, provisional:** D-089's existing fields can preserve the aggregate frame,
  source dependency, legal/practice split, near-anchor temporal state, status-term
  mappings and prevalence abstention without another contract extension. This is one
  researched case, not a tranche verdict and not independent review.
