# Hadhramaut — 500 BCE

Target: R1:L:COV2:-500:C. Frame: **polity**.
Research stage: **under_review**. Research outcome: **unassessed**.
Experimental packet, 2026-09-24; no independent historical review, canonical update,
P-level assignment, geometry promotion or publication.

## Bounded synthesis

The kingdom/frame is defensible around the 500 BCE anchor, but the presently accessible
target-specific evidence does **not** yet justify a territorial slavery designation for
Hadhramaut at that date.

Shabwa is securely associated with the ancient kingdom of Hadramawt during the first
millennium BCE, while the French archaeological synthesis explicitly cautions that the
chronology is difficult because historical texts are sparse and excavations at Shabwa
produced few inscriptions. The Raybūn-Hadrān Hadramitic corpus reaches the early part
of the first millennium BCE relevant to the anchor, but it is overwhelmingly terse
religious epigraphy and is not a slave-status register.

This is not evidence of absence. South Arabian epigraphy more broadly supplies strong
evidence for slavery, captivity, ownership and sale, and it also demonstrates why
subordinate-status vocabulary cannot be normalized mechanically. The problem is
**scope**: that regional evidence cannot be silently imported into Hadhramaut, and a
much later Hadramitic social-stratification inscription cannot be back-projected to
500 BCE.

The correct state for this packet is therefore **under_review / unassessed**. The
remaining uncertainty is source access and target-specific status evidence, not a
historical finding that slavery was absent.

No P-level is assigned.

## Identity and temporal frame

The frozen registry treats Hadhramaut as a polity. Shabwa functioned as the ancient
capital of the Hadramawt kingdom across the first millennium BCE, but the chronology
of early Hadramawt remains imprecise.

The official Shabwa archaeological synthesis notes:
- the oldest relevant evidence reaches roughly the seventh century BCE;
- Hadramitic-speaking communities and cult centers are present through the first
  millennium BCE;
- Shabwa's historical chronology is difficult because direct historical texts are
  sparse;
- commercial links become increasingly visible, but trade connectivity is not
  evidence of slavery.

The Raybūn-Hadrān publication describes 458 Hadramitic inscriptions ranging from the
fifth to first centuries BCE. François Bron's review of the same corpus instead says
the excavated temple appears to have been used from the fourth to first centuries BCE,
with chronology based on ceramics, palaeography and linguistic change.

Those are not silently harmonized. For this experiment they mean that the Hadrān
material is **near-anchor / early-period evidence with dating uncertainty**, not a
guaranteed exact 500 BCE cross-section.

## The strongest target-specific corpus: Raybūn-Hadrān

Frantsouzoff's 2001 edition publishes and comments on 458 inscriptions from one temple
at Raybūn. The AIBL summary makes an unusually important negative observation: the
Hadrān inscriptions, and Raybūn inscriptions more generally, do not mention central
power or indicate individuals' places in a social hierarchy in the way inscriptions
from other South Arabian kingdoms can.

The same summary immediately states that conclusions from this are an
**argumentum a silentio** and are not generally agreed. Bron's scholarly review likewise
emphasizes how fragmentary and formulaic the material is: most of the 458 entries are
very fragmentary, and the corpus is especially informative for language, cult and
onomastics rather than a complete social census.

Therefore:
- absence of an explicit slave-status marker in the accessible summary is **not**
  evidence that slavery was absent;
- archive genre and preservation restrict what the corpus can answer;
- no P0/P1/P2/P3/P4 inference may be manufactured from the silence;
- the corpus remains potentially important because the underlying inscriptions and
  specialist commentary may contain claim-specific status evidence not exposed by
  the public summary.

## Broader South Arabian slavery evidence: context, not territorial transfer

Valentina Grasso's 2023 specialist study shows that the South Arabian epigraphic
corpus contains multiple subordinate-status terms and explicit evidence of captivity,
purchase, ownership and sale. It is strong evidence that slavery and slave-like
property relations existed in ancient South Arabia.

It is **not** a Hadhramaut-at-500-BCE attestation.

Grasso's analysis is especially useful for category control:
- `ʿbd` is highly ambiguous and can express subordination to a deity or a human;
- `ʾdm` can mean a liegeman/subject and also occurs in religious subordination;
- `s¹byw` is used for captives;
- `ʾmt` / related female-status vocabulary can occur in proprietary contexts;
- texts such as Said 1 and X.BSB 61 provide explicit regional evidence for purchase
  or proprietary transfer of women;
- a specific Hadramitic inscription used by Grasso to illustrate social stratification
  dates only to the late second–late third centuries CE.

This regional corpus establishes a strong **background possibility and terminology
problem**, not territorial practice at the frozen Hadhramaut anchor.

## Ambiguous source-native lead: RES 4700

The British Museum records RES 4700 (museum 125687), reportedly from Shabwa, as a
three-line South Arabian inscription described as Hadramitic **or Sabaean**. Its
catalogue description calls Dhad-Karib son of Ma'ad-Karib a “servant” of Yada'il and
Yata'amar in a record concerning a well.

This is not promoted to slavery evidence because:
- the museum entry gives no secure date usable for the 500 BCE anchor;
- even its language classification is Hadramitic or Sabaean;
- “servant” is a translation of a subordinate relationship, not by itself proof of
  enslaved legal status;
- Grasso independently demonstrates that such status terms can be context-sensitive.

RES 4700 is therefore a **status-language lead / exclusion**, not a positive
slave-status attestation.

## Unread but potentially decisive Hadramitic scholarship

Several target-specific resources are known but were not fully accessible in this pass.

### Raybūn, Kafas/Naʿmān — 2007

Frantsouzoff's *Inventaire des inscriptions sudarabiques*, vol. VI, publishes another
large Raybūn temple corpus. Public metadata confirms the edition and site, but this pass
did not obtain searchable full text sufficient for a claim-specific slavery/status
audit.

### *East of Aden* — 2012

Frantsouzoff's 2012 monograph explicitly analyzes the epigraphic documentation,
religious life and **social structure** of Raybūn as a cult center within ancient
Hadramawt. The Institute of Oriental Manuscripts page confirms that scope and offers
summary/prefatory materials, but the relevant social-structure discussion was not
available here as searchable full text.

This is a genuine reopening source: it could contain either positive status evidence
or a stronger explanation of what the Raybūn corpus can and cannot support.

### Hadramitic onomastic index — 2026

Arbach and Frantsouzoff's 2026 *An onomastic index of the Ḥaḍramitic inscriptions*
covers the seventh century BCE through fourth century CE and includes roughly 1,500
proper names, social groups and a glossary of political/social figures. Public
bibliographic descriptions were accessible; the full index/glossary was not read.

The work is useful as a navigation/recovery source, not itself evidence for slavery
until exact entries are inspected.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci:
- Shabwa and Raybūn for target identity/context;
- Raybūn-Hadrān for an early Hadramitic corpus;
- broader South Arabia for regional slavery/category context only;
- later Hadramitic evidence for diachronic comparison only.

Maximum current inference:
**Hadhramaut is a defensible historical polity frame around the anchor, and slavery
is demonstrably part of the wider South Arabian evidence environment, but current
accessible target-specific evidence does not establish Hadhramaut territorial slavery
at 500 BCE.**

Required abstention:
- do not infer absence from Hadrān's silence or fragmentary/formulaic corpus;
- do not import Sabaic/Minaic/Qatabanian or generic South Arabian evidence into the
  Hadhramaut territorial claim;
- do not back-project late antique Hadramitic social evidence;
- do not equate `ʿbd`, `ʾdm` or translated “servant” automatically with slave;
- do not infer slavery from Shabwa's trade-network position;
- do not derive prevalence from inscription counts;
- do not let a modern or broad Hadramawt polygon strengthen the evidence.

## Law / practice and network / territory

No near-anchor Hadhramaut legal text was recovered that independently establishes a
slave-status regime. Regional proprietary texts are practice/legal evidence for their
own South Arabian loci and periods, not Hadhramaut law.

Shabwa's commercial connections are historically important, but participation in the
incense network does not establish slave trading or local enslavement. Network
participation and territorial practice stay separate.

## Dependency and access audit

- AIBL's Hadrān page summarizes Frantsouzoff 2001; it is not independent evidence
  from the underlying corpus.
- Bron 2005 is an independent scholarly review of the same published Hadrān corpus,
  useful for dating and genre/preservation limits, not another inscription set.
- Frantsouzoff 2012 synthesizes Raybūn material overlapping the 2001/2007 editions.
- Grasso 2023 is an independent regional slavery/category synthesis but reuses South

- The British Museum RES 4700 catalogue is a source-native object record but its
  date/language/status are insufficient for the target claim.
- The 2026 onomastic index is a navigation/reference layer over the Hadramitic corpus,

French and English summaries/reviews were read. No independent reading of Hadramitic
was performed. The underlying Hadrān/Kafas editions, *East of Aden* social-structure
chapter and 2026 index/glossary were not read in full. These access limits are
classification-relevant and remain explicit.

## Adversarial findings

- **REJECT:** broader South Arabian evidence means Hadhramaut can be colored positive
  at 500 BCE. Regional context is not territorial attestation.
- **REJECT:** Hadrān's lack of hierarchy markers proves slavery was absent. The
  publication itself calls this an argument from silence and notes lack of consensus.
- **REVISE:** the Hadrān corpus begins neatly at exactly 500 BCE. Publisher and review
  dating differ; retain near-anchor chronology uncertainty.
- **REJECT:** a Shabwa inscription translated “servant” proves slave status. Its date,
  language and category mapping are not sufficiently bounded.
- **REJECT:** Shabwa's major trade role implies slave-trade participation. No such
  inference follows from network centrality.
- **SURVIVES, provisional:** D-089 can preserve regional versus territorial scope,
  lexical ambiguity, source dependency, chronology uncertainty and a source-access
  blocker while leaving the target under review.
- **PARK:** absence/prevalence judgment until target-specific corpus recovery.
