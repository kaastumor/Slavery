# Viking trade network — 900 CE

Target: R1:L:viking_900. Frame: **mobile_network**, not one society, ethnicity,
state or territorial jurisdiction.
Research stage: **researched_internal**.
Research outcome: **bounded_supported_network_slaving**.
Experimental packet, 2026-09-24; no independent historical review, canonical update,
P-level assignment, geometry promotion or publication.

## Bounded synthesis

The frozen target is unusually well matched to the evidence. Around the 900 CE
anchor, contemporary/near-contemporary sources and specialist synthesis support
captive-taking, enslavement and sale as activities within parts of the wider Viking/
Rūs commercial-raiding network.

Ibn Rustah, writing c. 903–913, describes Rūs groups attacking the Saqaliba by ship,
taking captives and carrying them to Khazar and Volga Bulgar markets for sale. This is
a near-anchor source directly describing capture, transport and sale across a mobile
riverine network.

Independent western evidence documents Viking captive-taking throughout the ninth
century. The Annals of Ulster record the capture of many women at Étar in 821, and
specialist synthesis shows repeated detention and transport of captives to ships and
encampments. These western records are evidence for capture practices among Viking
raiding groups, although they do not prove that every captive was sold or that eastern
and western routes formed one centrally organized market.

Archaeological finds of probable shackles/collars at nodes such as Birka, Hedeby and
Dublin are compatible with captive movement and sale, but the artifacts are individually
ambiguous. They reinforce a historically attested network interpretation; they do not
by themselves quantify trade volume or prove a dedicated slave market at each site.

The network result is therefore positive and bounded: **slaving and human trafficking
were documented activities within multiple Viking/Rūs raiding and trade circuits around
the 900 CE period.** This does not mean every Viking group traded enslaved people, every
market was a slave market, or every Scandinavian territory practiced slavery at the
same level.

No territorial P-level is assigned to the mobile network.

## Identity and frame

“Viking trade network” is not a polity.

The relevant actors include heterogeneous Scandinavian raiders, merchants, settlers
and Rūs groups operating across different political and cultural environments. Rūs
identity in Arabic and Byzantine sources cannot be reduced mechanically to modern
nationality or to one homogeneous Scandinavian ethnic category.

Accordingly:
- evidence belongs to routes, groups, ports and transactions;
- a Viking/Rūs source does not transfer a territorial practice classification to
  Scandinavia, Ireland, the Slavic regions, Khazaria or Volga Bulgaria;
- the people being trafficked cannot have nationality inferred from broad source labels
  such as Saqaliba beyond the source's own category and specialist interpretation.

## Near-anchor eastern route evidence

### Ibn Rustah, c. 903–913

Ibn Rustah's description is unusually valuable for the frozen anchor. In the standard
modern translation tradition, the Rūs travel by ship against the Saqaliba, take them
captive, carry them to Khazar and Bulgar territory and sell them.

This establishes a bounded chain:
**raiding → captivity → transport → sale**.

It does not establish:
- exact numbers;
- a single permanent market organization;
- the personal identity or precise origin of every captive;
- that all Rūs income came from slaves rather than furs and other trade;
- a territorial institution at the captives' place of origin.

The source is a geographical ethnographic account, not a merchant ledger, so the
specialist interpretation remains important.

### Ibn Fadlan, 921–922: succeeding-period confirmation

Ibn Fadlan's Volga account describes Rūs merchants with enslaved young women and a
funeral involving an enslaved woman. It is two decades after the anchor and therefore
not an exact 900 CE observation, but it independently confirms that captive/slave
ownership and commerce remained embedded in Rūs riverine activity shortly afterward.

It is used as succeeding-period continuity, not backdated as an anchor event.

## Western ninth-century captive-taking

Raffield's source-critical work assembles contemporary western annals documenting
Viking capture practices. Examples include:
- women taken at Étar in 821;
- repeated holding of captives at ships/encampments in Ireland during the 820s–840s;
- threats to take women and children in Frisia in 873;
- other ninth-century raids in which captives were taken for ransom, exploitation or
  likely sale.

These records independently demonstrate that captivity was not confined to the eastern
Rūs routes.

However, **capture is not automatically slave sale**. High-status prisoners could be
ransomed, and source genres rarely record the eventual fate of every captive. The
packet therefore separates captive-taking from confirmed sale wherever the source does.

## Trade nodes and archaeological evidence

Raffield 2019 reviews possible iron shackles/collars from Birka, Hedeby, Dublin and
other Viking-Age sites. Concentration at major trading nodes, including examples from
Hedeby's harbour, is consistent with captive transport and sale.

The material evidence remains cautious:
- restraint objects can have alternative functions;
- no large dedicated slave-market compound has been securely identified;
- market architecture need not be permanent;
- silver/dirham inflows are an economic proxy, not a headcount of enslaved people.

This archaeological ambiguity matters because otherwise the unusually rich Viking
material record could be turned into false precision.

## Wider trade-system interpretation

Jankowiak's work on Saqaliba and early Islamic slave trade combines Arabic written
sources with the enormous movement of dirhams into northern/eastern Europe. It supports
a substantial long-distance slave-trade component in exchanges linking northern
Europe, eastern Europe and Islamic markets.

His later work stresses logistical complexity and multiple operating models rather
than a single unified pipeline.

Accordingly:
- Islamic silver is relevant corroborating economic context;
- a dirham hoard is not a receipt for a particular enslaved person;
- coin volume cannot mechanically become a slave count or prevalence estimate;
- furs and other commodities were also traded.

## Historical terms and category mapping

Relevant terms/categories include:
- Old Norse **þræll / thrall**, best documented through later Scandinavian legal,
  literary, linguistic and runic evidence;
- Arabic **Saqaliba**, a broad ethnogeographic label for Slavic populations and
  sometimes neighboring peoples, not a precise nationality tag;
- captives/prisoners, some of whom were ransomed rather than enslaved;
- concubines/enslaved women in Arabic accounts;
- Rūs, an actor label whose ethnic composition and political meaning vary by source.

Brink's synthesis warns that there are no contemporary Scandinavian-written sources
that straightforwardly describe everyday Viking-Age slave life, and later sagas/laws
must be handled critically. The network conclusion therefore rests primarily on
contemporary external sources plus archaeology and specialist synthesis, not on
uncritical saga projection.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci:
- eastern river systems linking Rūs, Slavic regions, Khazar and Volga Bulgar markets;
- western Viking raiding zones in Ireland/Frisia and associated ship camps;
- trading nodes such as Birka, Hedeby and Dublin as archaeological context.

Maximum defensible inference:
**around the late ninth/early tenth century, parts of the wider Viking/Rūs mobile
network demonstrably engaged in capture, transport and sale of people, with independent
western evidence for extensive captive-taking and eastern near-anchor evidence for
explicit slave sale.**

Required abstention:
- no one “Viking state” or homogeneous Viking society;
- no automatic territorial practice inference for Scandinavia or connected regions;
- no assumption that every captive was enslaved or sold;
- no nationality inference from Saqaliba, Rūs or personal origin labels;
- no trade volume from dirham/shackle counts;
- no automatic slave-market label for every port;
- no prevalence or population-share estimate.

## Law / practice and network / territory

This target is primarily **network participation**, not territorial law.

No single Viking legal regime governs the evidence. Later Scandinavian laws can
illuminate thraldom but cannot substitute for c. 900 practice across a mobile network.
Conversely, explicit capture-and-sale reports establish practice without proving the
law of every polity through which captives passed.

## Dependency and access audit

- Ibn Rustah is one near-anchor source; modern translations and later scholarship
  quoting him are one source chain, not multiple attestations.
- Ibn Fadlan is a separate eyewitness/travel account from 921–922 and is succeeding
  continuity, not exact anchor evidence.
- Irish/Frankish annal entries are independent western source traditions for captive
  taking; later syntheses quoting them do not multiply independence.
- Raffield 2019/2022 reuses many of those written sources and archaeological finds;
  it is used for synthesis and source-role control rather than row-count corroboration.
- Brink 2021 is a broad specialist synthesis and terminology/source-criticism layer.
- Jankowiak's dirham/slave-trade work synthesizes written and numismatic evidence; coin
  data remain proxy context rather than direct captive records.

Modern specialist literature and translated primary-source excerpts were reviewed.
Arabic originals and Old Norse manuscript traditions were not independently edited.

## Adversarial findings

- **REJECT:** “Viking network” means one people or polity.
- **REJECT:** every recorded captive was sold as a slave; ransom and other outcomes
  existed.
- **REJECT:** dirhams/shackles can be counted into slave totals.
- **REJECT:** a port connected to Viking trade automatically becomes a slave market.
- **REVISE:** later sagas/laws alone establish c. 900 practice; contemporary external
  evidence must carry the anchor claim.
- **SURVIVES, provisional:** D-089 handles this non-polity case by keeping network
  participation separate from territorial practice and preserving source role,
  chronology and category ambiguity.
- **SUPPORTED:** unlike the polity cases, the frozen network frame itself can carry a
  positive bounded result without inventing a territorial classification.
