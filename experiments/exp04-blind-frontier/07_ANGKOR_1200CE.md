# Angkor — 1200 CE

Research stage: **researched_internal**.
Research outcome: **bounded_supported_near_anchor_with_category_boundary**.
Experimental packet, 2026-09-24; no independent historical review, canonical update,
P-level assignment, geometry promotion or publication.

## Bounded synthesis

Angkor at the 1200 CE anchor has unusually strong near-anchor evidence for large,
institutionalized systems of dependent and unfree labor centered on royal-temple
foundations.

Jayavarman VII's Ta Prohm inscription K.273 (1186) and Preah Khan inscription K.908
(1191) are only fourteen and nine years before the anchor. They document enormous
temple establishments, assigned villages, personnel and provisioning obligations.
Specialist analysis of Angkorian inscriptions shows that many non-elite personnel were
bonded through mechanisms including inheritance, punishment, captivity and possibly
debt.

The historical category boundary is nevertheless essential. Older scholarship often
translated long temple personnel lists simply as “slaves.” More recent work shows that
Old Khmer `khñum` and related appellations encompassed people with different roles
and statuses. Some inscriptional contexts explicitly support property/bondage readings;
other workers were cyclical laborers, servants, specialists or people attached through
village obligations.

The bounded conclusion is therefore:
**institutionalized unfree/bonded labor is strongly supported at Angkor immediately
before 1200, including statuses reasonably mapped to slavery in specific inscriptional
contexts, but not every person counted in temple service can be classified as a slave.**

This is a node/site conclusion around Angkor's royal-temple complex. It is not an
empire-wide prevalence estimate.

No P-level is assigned in EXP-04.

## Identity and temporal frame

The frozen target is **Angkor**, a capital/site complex.

Ta Prohm and Preah Khan are royal foundations inside the Angkor landscape and are
excellent evidence loci for the node. Their assigned villages and provisioning systems
extend beyond the monument enclosures, but those networks do not transform the
archaeological park or later metropolitan footprint into the political extent of the
Khmer Empire.

Temporal fit is strong but still stated honestly:
- K.273: consecration 1186 CE;
- K.908: consecration 1191 CE;
- anchor: 1200 CE.

These are **near-anchor** institutional records, not documents dated exactly to 1200.

## Ta Prohm K.273 — 1186

K.273 records the Rajavihara/Ta Prohm foundation under Jayavarman VII. The inscription
enumerates personnel, villages, goods and provisioning at very large scale. Modern
archaeological/specialist syntheses report roughly 12,640 people directly associated
with temple functions and tens of thousands more in villages assigned to its support.

The raw totals do not equal a slave count.

The inscription is important because it demonstrates the administrative capacity to
bind settlements and personnel to a religious foundation. Cœdès' original publication
also noted Cham personnel in the temple establishment, plausibly prisoners from the
king's wars, but the packet does not infer captive status for every Cham-named person.

## Preah Khan K.908 — 1191

K.908 describes another enormous Jayavarman VII foundation. It records thousands of
villages and large numbers of temple personnel/provisioners tied to the institution.

Again, the evidentiary point is institutional scale and attachment, not that every
named or counted person was enslaved.

Together K.273 and K.908 are separate royal foundation inscriptions but belong to the
same reign, administrative milieu and temple-economic system. They are multiple
attestations, not independent samples of the population.

## Old Khmer category problem

Lustig and Lustig's analysis of more than 100 Angkorian inscriptions and over 17,000
individually identified personnel shows why the old “slave lists” label is too blunt.

Angkorian commoners could be described by terms including `khñum`, Sanskrit
`dāsa/dāsī`, `si`, `gho`, `tai` and occupational/status labels. Specific texts
can describe people as property of a divinity, hereditary hands, purchased persons,
field workers, servants or sanctuary workers.

Important distinctions:
- `khñum vraḥ` (“khñum of the god/divinity”) can occur in explicit property and
  temple-bondage contexts;
- `khñum` alone should not automatically be normalized to modern chattel slavery;
- sanctuary/non-sanctuary personnel had different roles and status histories;
- villages could be donated or assigned to maintain temples, binding communities to
  institutional obligations without making every villager a slave;
- cyclical workgroups and corvée-like obligations coexisted with more permanently
  bonded people.

The Atlas therefore records category mapping at the claim/source level rather than
turning one translation convention into a universal type.

## Captivity, purchase and inheritance

Specialist synthesis indicates multiple pathways into dependent/unfree statuses in
Angkorian society, including:
- inheritance;
- punishment;
- war/tribal capture;
- possible debt;
- purchase/property transactions in certain inscriptions;
- donation/assignment of people or communities to foundations.

Those mechanisms overlap but are not interchangeable. A war captive is not
automatically proven to remain a slave for life; a village labor obligation is not the
same as individual property status.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci:
- Ta Prohm/Rajavihara;
- Preah Khan/Jayasri;
- other Angkorian temple inscriptions used for status/category comparison;
- assigned villages/provisioning networks linked to those institutions.

Maximum defensible inference:
**near 1200, Angkor's royal-temple economy demonstrably incorporated large,
institutionalized systems of bonded/unfree labor, with specific inscriptional contexts
supporting slavery/property status and others requiring broader dependency or labor
labels.**

Required abstention:
- no equation of all temple personnel with slaves;
- no slave totals derived from K.273/K.908 personnel totals;
- no empire-wide prevalence from Angkor's unusually rich epigraphy;
- no assumption that every assigned village inhabitant was personally unfree;
- no automatic captive→slave→hereditary status chain;
- no archaeological-park polygon as imperial or practice extent.

## Law / practice and network / territory

The inscriptions are foundation/administrative records and thus provide direct practice
and institutional evidence, but they do not constitute one comprehensive Khmer slave
code.

War captives and imported/ethnically labelled personnel introduce a network dimension,
but origin or ethnicity does not establish the legal status of each person. The
territorial result remains bounded to Angkor's institutional node and linked
provisioning system.

## Geometry state

**point_site_or_reviewed_bounded_geometry; historical practice extent unresolved.**

The temples are locatable sites. Their modern archaeological enclosures are not the
extent of the labor system, while the wider Angkor metropolitan zone is not identical
to Khmer political jurisdiction. A view may show the node and linked evidence loci
without coloring the whole empire.

## Dependency and access audit

- K.273 and Cœdès 1906 are one source chain; later numerical/archaeological studies
  reuse the same inscription.
- K.908 is a separate inscription from the same reign/system; modern translations and
  museum summaries do not multiply its independence.
- Lustig/Lustig synthesize a much broader inscriptional corpus and are used primarily
  for category/status interpretation and anti-counting controls.
- Archaeological population/provisioning studies reuse epigraphic totals but provide
  independent spatial/economic modeling; they do not add new slave-status attestations.
- Later Zhou Daguan evidence is useful for late-13th-century comparison but is not
  required for the near-anchor result and is not backdated.

English/French specialist scholarship and published translations/editions were
reviewed. Old Khmer and Sanskrit were not independently re-edited by this pass.

## Adversarial findings

- **REJECT:** K.273's or K.908's personnel totals are slave totals.
- **REJECT:** `khñum = slave` universally.
- **REJECT:** monumental construction or large labor mobilization alone proves slavery;
  the positive claim depends on inscriptional status/property evidence.
- **REJECT:** Angkor node evidence automatically supports empire-wide prevalence.
- **REVISE:** war-captive ethnic labels establish each individual's slave status.
- **SURVIVES, provisional:** D-089 preserves site/node scope, category mapping,
  institutional practice, source dependency and anti-prevalence constraints.
- **SUPPORTED:** a bounded near-anchor institutional unfree/slavery claim is defensible
  without forcing all temple labor into one category.
