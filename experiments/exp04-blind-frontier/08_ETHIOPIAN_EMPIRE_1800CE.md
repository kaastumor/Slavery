## Bounded synthesis

Slavery and slave trading are strongly supported in the Ethiopian region around the
1800 CE anchor. Near-anchor travel evidence from the late Gondarine period documents
the sale and export of enslaved children, enslaved people in Gondar households and
palace service, and trade routes connecting the highlands to Massawa and neighboring
Muslim markets. Specialist histories support continuation of domestic slavery and
interregional trade into the early nineteenth century.

The political frame is the essential qualification. In 1800 the Solomonic imperial
institution persisted during the **Zemene Mesafint**, but effective sovereignty was
fragmented among regional lords. Fernyhough notes that large-scale central-state
participation in acquiring and marketing captives declined during this period even as
slavery and slave trading continued through regional and private actors.

The correct result is therefore:
**slavery and slave trading were institutionally present in multiple parts of the
Ethiopian region near 1800, but no uniform empire-wide practice, intensity or central
policy may be inferred from the imperial title.**

No P-level is assigned in EXP-04.

## Identity and sovereignty frame

The frozen registry deliberately marks the target
**imperial_title_fragmented_sovereignty**.

The Zemene Mesafint, beginning in the late eighteenth century and continuing to the
mid-nineteenth, saw emperors retain dynastic/religious significance while regional
lords exercised much effective power across Tigray, Gojjam, Wollo/Yejju, Shewa and
other regions.

Accordingly:
- “Ethiopian Empire” at 1800 is not one uniformly governed territory;
- an attestation in Gondar/Tigray does not automatically establish identical practice
  in every region;
- evidence from southern polities not yet incorporated into the later empire must not
  be back-projected into the 1800 imperial territory;
- later Menelik-era conquests cannot define the 1800 spatial frame.

## Near-anchor primary evidence: James Bruce

James Bruce travelled in Ethiopia in 1768–1773 and published his account in 1790,
placing his observations roughly one generation before the anchor.

At Dixan he described a trade in kidnapped children: Christian actors brought children
taken in Abyssinia to the frontier town, where Muslim merchants carried them onward
to Massawa and thence Arabia or India. Bruce also described religious/political actors'
involvement and payments connected to the traffic.

Specialist synthesis of Bruce's Gondar evidence reports numerous enslaved people in
elite and royal service during the second half of the eighteenth century, including
young people educated for palace/household service and enslaved cavalry.

Bruce is a foreign traveler and his rhetoric requires source criticism. Nevertheless,
the observations are bounded practice evidence and are close enough to the anchor to
serve as near-anchor predecessor evidence when combined with early-nineteenth-century
continuity.

## Early nineteenth-century continuity

Pankhurst's social/economic synthesis and later specialist slavery scholarship document
domestic slavery across northern/central Ethiopia in the early nineteenth century.
Enslaved women were commonly employed in household labor; chiefs and wealthy families
held enslaved dependents; Gondar remained linked to long-distance routes through which
slaves, ivory, gold and other goods moved.

Fernyhough's regional history is especially important for the sovereignty distinction.
He argues that Gondarine rulers before the Zemene Mesafint had raided, traded and taxed
slaves, while large-scale state involvement declined during the fragmented
1765–1855 period. Slavery and commerce in captives nevertheless persisted through
other actors and regions.

This prevents two opposite errors:
- fragmentation does not mean slavery disappeared;
- persistent slavery does not mean a strong central imperial slave policy existed.

## Law and legal status

The **Fetha Nagast**, used in Christian Ethiopia, contains a dedicated body of rules
on slavery and manumission. It treated enslaved status as legally recognizable while
also setting conditions and restrictions around ownership, sale and emancipation.

A sixteenth-century royal edict against trafficking Christian slaves to non-Christian
buyers likewise sought to regulate who could be enslaved/sold rather than abolishing
slavery itself.

These older legal materials establish a durable normative tradition but are not proof
of uniform enforcement in 1800. The Zemene Mesafint's fragmented political authority
makes the law/practice distinction particularly important.

## Historical terms and category mapping

Ethiopian dependency terminology is not safely reducible to one English label.

Specialist work distinguishes terms including:
- **barya** / historically associated slave-status language;
- Geʿez **gabr/gaber** in contexts that may mean servant/slave;
- **gäbbar/gabar**, often tributary peasantry/commoner status rather than chattel
  slavery;
- female servant/slave terms such as **ʿamat** and **garad** in historical usage;
- **shifta**, soldiers/raiders and other actors who could participate in capture, not
  a slave-status term.

Meckelburg's work explicitly warns that `gabr` and related terms blur servant,
tributary and slave meanings in different contexts. The Atlas must therefore preserve
source-specific mappings rather than normalize every dependent peasant as enslaved.

## Acquisition, trade and labor roles

Specialist synthesis identifies multiple pathways into enslavement in the Ethiopian/
Eritrean region:
- warfare and raiding;
- kidnapping;
- debt;
- birth into enslaved status;
- market purchase/trade.

Enslaved people were used in domestic service, agriculture, elite/palace households
and other occupations, while some were exported along Red Sea/Sudan routes.

These pathways and roles are evidence of institutional presence but cannot be counted
into a single prevalence figure from surviving travel narratives or market reports.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci:
- Gondar and northern/central highlands for household/palace slavery;
- Tigray/Dixan/Massawa route for near-anchor child capture/export;
- regional trade routes through Gojjam and toward Sudan/Red Sea;
- broader Ethiopian/Eritrean specialist synthesis for institutional continuity.

Maximum defensible inference:
**slavery and slave trading were established practices in multiple northern/central
Ethiopian contexts around the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, while
effective sovereignty in 1800 was fragmented and practice cannot be assumed uniform
throughout the nominal empire.**

Required abstention:
- no one homogeneous 1800 “Ethiopian” practice level;
- no later imperial borders back-projected to 1800;
- no automatic slave mapping for gäbbar/tributary/servant statuses;
- no ethnicity or nationality inferred from labels applied to enslaved populations;
- no prevalence from traveler estimates, market counts or later nineteenth-century
  export volumes;
- no conflation of slave trade law with actual enforcement.

## Network / territorial separation

The external slave trade through Massawa, Sudan and other routes is positively
supported but remains a **network dimension**.

People could be captured in one region, sold in another and exported through a third.
The place of sale or export does not establish where enslavement occurred, while an
origin label does not establish political identity.

Domestic territorial practice and long-distance trade therefore remain separate but
coexisting dimensions.

## Dependency and access audit

- Bruce is one near-anchor traveler source; Pankhurst's quotations/synthesis of Bruce
  are dependent on that source chain.
- Pankhurst combines many nineteenth-century travelers/missionaries; individual later
  reports are not mechanically counted as independent anchor attestations.
- Fernyhough focuses heavily on southern Ethiopia and later nineteenth-century trade;
  his value here is the Zemene Mesafint state-participation distinction and regional
  chronology, not back-projecting southern evidence.
- Meckelburg and current Oxford syntheses combine broad historiography and are used for
  terminology/institutional structure.
- Fetha Nagast and the 1548 edict are legal-context chains far earlier than the anchor,
  not near-anchor practice records.

English translations and specialist syntheses were reviewed. Geʿez/Amharic legal
manuscripts and Bruce's source languages were not independently re-edited.

## Adversarial findings

- **REJECT:** nominal Ethiopian imperial continuity means uniform territorial practice
  in 1800.
- **REJECT:** Zemene Mesafint fragmentation means slavery was merely peripheral or
  absent.
- **REJECT:** gäbbar/gabr/servant terminology can be globally normalized to slave.
- **REVISE:** nineteenth-century southern Ethiopian slavery automatically belongs
  inside the 1800 imperial frame; much of that territory was incorporated later.
- **REJECT:** slave export estimates can determine prevalence or P-level.
- **SURVIVES, provisional:** D-089 preserves strong slavery evidence alongside
  fragmented sovereignty, regional loci, network participation, category ambiguity
  and legal/practice separation.
- **SUPPORTED:** bounded institutional presence around the anchor is defensible without
