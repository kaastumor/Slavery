# EXP-04 case 5 — Viking trade network, 900 CE

**Target:** R1:L:viking_900
**Frame:** mobile_network
**State:** experimental research packet
**Research outcome:** network_slaving_strongly_supported; territorial_practice_not_inferred
**Canonical effect:** none

## Bounded proposition

Specialist historical and archaeological scholarship strongly supports captive-taking, trafficking and sale of enslaved people as part of interconnected Viking-Age exchange networks in the ninth and tenth centuries.

This is a network-participation claim. It does not create one territorial-practice classification for “the Viking world” or for every settlement connected to the network.

## Required abstention

Do not:
- recolor Scandinavia, Ireland, the Rus river routes, or every trading port as a territorial slavery zone solely from network evidence;
- infer slave-market scale from the small surviving corpus of shackles;
- treat Arabic descriptions of Rus merchants as direct evidence for every Scandinavian society;
- treat later saga evidence as straightforward contemporary observation;
- use silver/dirham distributions alone as a proxy for slave-trade volume.

## Evidence locus / inference extent

**Evidence locus:** written accounts of raids/captive taking, trading nodes including Dublin/Birka/Hedeby, and eastern Rus routes.

**Maximum inference:** slaving was a real and interconnected Viking-Age network activity around the anchor period. Local territorial practice requires separate evidence.

## Historical terminology / mapping

Relevant terms include Old Norse thraldom/thrall and source-specific terms for captives/enslaved people.

**Mapping status:** direct_or_well_established for slavery as a network relation; local social statuses remain variable.

## Source roles

- Ben Raffield, “The slave markets of the Viking world: comparative perspectives on an ‘invisible archaeology’,” Slavery & Abolition (2019), DOI 10.1080/0144039X.2019.1592976. Role: specialist network/archaeological synthesis. Strong for raiding, captive trade and nodal-market interpretation; explicitly warns that archaeological traces are fragmentary.
- Stefan Brink, Thraldom: A History of Slavery in the Viking Age, Oxford University Press (2021), DOI 10.1093/oso/9780197532355.001.0001. Role: specialist synthesis of slavery/dependency and source limitations.
- Poul Holm, “The Slave Trade of Dublin, Ninth to Twelfth Centuries,” Peritia 5 (1986), DOI 10.1484/J.Peri.3.139. Role: Dublin-specific historical study; strong for ninth-century captive-taking and later market growth, not a universal Scandinavian practice estimate.
- Ibn Fadlan’s c. 921 account, used through modern specialist analysis. Role: near-contemporary primary witness to Rus merchants with enslaved women; geographically specific and culturally mediated.

## Counter/limiting evidence

Raffield emphasizes that dedicated slave markets are archaeologically difficult to identify; restraints are sparse and multi-use interpretation requires caution. Brink likewise stresses serious source problems for reconstructing everyday Viking-Age slave life.
