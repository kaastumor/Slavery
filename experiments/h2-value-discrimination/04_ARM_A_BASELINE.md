## Q1 — Mexica / Nahua `tlacotli`

### Conventional synthesis

Calling `tlacotli` simply “Aztec slavery” is serviceable only as a first translation, not as a complete classification. Colonial dictionaries and the Florentine Codex tradition do translate `tlacotli` as `esclavo`, and specialist syntheses describe real unfreedom: people could enter the status through debt or punishment and another person controlled their labor.

But the status does not map neatly onto an inherited chattel model. Smith emphasizes that it was not hereditary and that `tlacotin` could marry, have free children and own property. Salazar goes further, arguing from Nahuatl/Spanish source comparison that many `tlacotli` relations pledged labor rather than ownership of the person, with retained freedom/personhood and possible exit after repayment. Von Mentz, however, stresses that some pledged people were reified, carried exchange value and could be bought or sold, which makes a purely benign contractual reading inadequate.

The safest conclusion is therefore plural and qualified: **Mexica society contained institutionalized forms of severe dependent or unfree labor commonly translated as slavery, but the category `tlacotli` should not be assumed to have all properties of a single modern slavery model.** Alienability, heredity, property rights, family rights, debt/punitive origins and special subcategories must be examined separately.

### Ordinary notes a competent researcher would keep

| Question | Working note |
| --- | --- |
| Is “slave” a historical translation? | Yes; sixteenth-century Spanish/Nahuatl traditions use it. |
| Is the translation analytically exact? | No; recent specialist work shows category substitution and heterogeneous indigenous statuses. |
| Was status hereditary? | General `tlacotli` described by Smith/Salazar: no; children free. |
| Could people own property/marry? | Yes in the syntheses consulted. |
| Was labor controlled by another? | Yes. |
| Could people be commodified? | Von Mentz says some relations involved market value/buying-selling; Salazar qualifies alienability for the general category and distinguishes special cases. |
| Best label | “`tlacotli` / severe dependent-unfree labor, conventionally translated ‘slave’; category-qualified.” |
| Main danger | Importing hereditary chattel assumptions from the English word “slave.” |

### Baseline limitation

The prose carries the nuance well. The main maintenance risk is that later comparative work can easily extract “slave” from the first sentence and forget which properties were explicitly qualified.

## Q2 — East India Company territories around 7 April 1843

### Conventional synthesis

Act V of 1843 provides a precise legal event. It instructed Company public officers and courts not to sell persons/right to compulsory labor on the ground of slavery or enforce alleged property rights in another person's person/services as a slave, protected a person's property from being denied because of slave status, and required crimes against an allegedly enslaved person to be treated as crimes against a free person.

That is a strong basis for saying that **legal recognition/enforcement of slave-property claims changed on 7 April 1843 in the territories to which the Act applied**. It is not a strong basis for saying that slavery or coercive dependency ceased in practice that day.

Major describes the measure as delegalisation rather than an act that actively freed every enslaved person, and emphasizes substantial regional/social variation in Indian slavery. Karak's Bengal study likewise argues that legal non-recognition did not amount to active emancipation and shows slavery embedded in overlapping caste/labor controls. The recent South Asia synthesis explicitly notes persistence after 1843, often under altered names/forms.

A conventional map or timeline should therefore separate the 1843 legal breakpoint from any practice chronology. It should also avoid coloring all of modern India from evidence concerning Company territory or Bengal.

### Ordinary notes a competent researcher would keep

| Question | Working note |
| --- | --- |
| Exact legal date | 7 April 1843 |
| Exact legal scope | East India Company territories as expressed in the Act; not a timeless modern-India polygon |
| What changed | Court/official enforcement and recognition of slave-property claims; equal penal protection provision |
| Did everyone become free in practice? | Not established; specialist scholarship says no active universal emancipation |
| Did coercive labor disappear? | No defensible basis for that inference; evidence indicates persistence/renaming and overlapping labor controls |
| Best visualization | Two lanes: legal status and observed/researched practice |
| Main danger | A single “abolished 1843” map color creating a false practice stop date |

### Baseline limitation

A careful timeline/table already prevents most of the obvious error. The challenge is whether those two lanes remain separate when the material is later combined with other places and dates.

## Q3 — Genoese Black Sea slave-trade network

### Conventional synthesis

From the late thirteenth through fifteenth centuries, Genoese commercial and political structures were deeply involved in Black Sea slave trading. Barker identifies Caffa as a central Genoese-administered hub and reconstructs an interregional trade connecting Black Sea supply zones to Mediterranean markets. The evidence includes state regulation, taxes, shipping, notarial sales and specific transport events.

That establishes **external/network participation** very strongly. It also establishes slave-market and transit activity at specific nodes such as Caffa.

It does not by itself tell us the prevalence of slavery inside Genoa. For that proposition a different evidence package is needed. Schiel supplies it: independent western-Mediterranean evidence documents significant slave sales in Genoa and enslaved people in urban households and work settings. Even then, the appropriate characterization is bounded; Barker explicitly frames Genoa and comparable societies as “societies with slaves,” not economies whose entire social structure can be inferred from route volume.

The chronological boundary also matters. Barker's book runs to 1500, but Genoese Caffa ended with Ottoman conquest in 1475. A network diagram labelled “Genoese Caffa, 1260–1500” would silently overextend political control.

### Ordinary notes a competent researcher would keep

| Proposition | Evidence type | Scope |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Genoese actors participated in Black Sea slave trade | trade/notarial/tax/state evidence | network |
| Caffa was a major Genoese slave-trade hub | port/administrative/trade evidence | Caffa until 1475 |
| Enslaved people were transported Caffa → Genoa | event/route evidence | specific movements |
| Slavery existed in Genoa city | separate household/sale/tax/notarial scholarship | Genoa urban practice |
| Slave-trade volume proves Genoese territorial prevalence | unsupported inference | must not assert |
| Genoese Caffa remains valid to 1500 | false | control ends 1475 |

### Baseline limitation

A competent table can keep network and territorial-practice propositions separate. A spatial/network view might reduce the effort needed to see locus, route and endpoint differences—but only if it does not visually imply that all connected places share the same practice intensity.
