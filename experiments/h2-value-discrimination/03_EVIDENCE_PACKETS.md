## Q1 — Mexica / Nahua `tlacotli`, c. 1400–1521

### E1 — Salazar Delgado 2025

Óscar Salazar Delgado, “Tlacotli: nuevos apuntes para la definición de una categoría nahua,” *Estudios de Cultura Náhuatl* 69 (2025), 139–174.

- URL: https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?pid=S0071-16752025000100139&script=sci_arttext
- Class: specialist scholarship using sixteenth-/seventeenth-century documentary sources.
- Relevant propositions:
  - colonial Spanish `esclavo` was used for more than one indigenous category, so translation itself does not establish one homogeneous status;
  - for the general `tlacotli` category discussed, the author emphasizes retained property/family rights, non-hereditary status, and an obligation centered on labor rather than ownership of the person;
  - the paper explicitly treats category reconstruction as difficult because many sources are only in Spanish.
- Direction: **qualifies/challenges** an undifferentiated modern “slave” label; **supports** a historically specific dependent-labor status.
- Limit: the argument should not be generalized automatically to every person whom colonial texts called `esclavo`.

### E2 — von Mentz 2007

Brígida von Mentz, “Esclavitud y semiesclavitud en el México Antiguo y en la Nueva España (con énfasis en el siglo XVI),” *Studia Historica. Historia Antigua* 25 (2007), 543–558.

- URL: https://gredos.usal.es/handle/10366/73848
- Class: specialist secondary scholarship.
- Relevant propositions:
  - Spaniards encountered subordinated people whom they called “slaves,” including Nahuatl `tlacotin`;
  - the article frames the evidence as a complex coexistence of slavery and semi-slavery/unfree labor rather than one simple category;
  - in the discussion of pledged `tlacotin`, von Mentz stresses reification, market exchange/value, and labor obligations.
- Direction: **challenges/qualifies** a purely contractual-free-labor reading; **supports** severe commodifying features in at least some `tlacotli` relations.
- Limit: the paper itself emphasizes heterogeneous labor conditions and does not justify treating every subordinated Nahua category as identical.

### E3 — Smith 2012 excerpt

Michael E. Smith, *The Aztecs*, 3rd ed. (Wiley-Blackwell, 2012), pp. 141–142, excerpted by Teaching Medieval Slavery and Captivity.

- URL: https://medievalslavery.org/mesoamerica/source-aztec-slaves/
- Class: specialist archaeological/historical synthesis.
- Relevant propositions:
  - uses “slaves / tlacotin” as an English shorthand;
  - describes debt and punishment as routes into status;
  - says the status was not hereditary and that enslaved people could marry, have free children and own property;
  - owners controlled labor and were responsible for subsistence.
- Direction: **supports** serious unfreedom while **qualifying** assumptions imported from hereditary chattel models.

### E4 — historical-language/source attestation

Nahuatl Dictionary (Wired Humanities), entry `tlacotli`, incorporating Molina 1571, Lockhart, Karttunen and Florentine Codex attestations.

- URL: https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/content/tlacotli
- Class: source/lexical aggregation with primary attestations.
- Relevant proposition:
  - colonial dictionaries commonly glossed `tlacotli` as `esclavo/esclava`;
  - the entry preserves multiple documentary attestations rather than proving that the European and Nahua categories were analytically identical.
- Direction: **supports** historical translation practice; **does not by itself support** a modern slavery-classification conclusion.

### Counter-pressure checked

The evidence packet contains a genuine interpretive tension: Salazar's recent reconstruction emphasizes retained freedom/personhood for the general category, while von Mentz emphasizes commodification/reification in at least some `tlacotli` relations. Smith's synthesis combines the English “slave” label with rights/status features unlike hereditary chattel slavery.

### Frozen baseline proposition

A responsible synthesis may say that Mexica/Nahua society contained recognized forms of severe dependent/unfree labor commonly translated as `tlacotli = slave`, including debt/punitive pathways and control over labor. It should **not** flatten all `tlacotli` or all colonial uses of “slave” into a single modern chattel-slavery category. Property, heredity, alienability, exit and ritual/penal subcategories require separate qualification.

## Q2 — East India Company territories around Act V of 1843

### E1 — Act V of 1843

*The Indian Slavery Act, 1843*, Act No. V of 1843, 7 April 1843.

- Official text: https://www.indiacode.nic.in/repealedfileopen?rfilename=A1843-05.pdf
- Class: primary legal source.
- Relevant propositions:
  - public officers were not to sell persons/right to compulsory labor on the ground of slavery;
  - civil/criminal courts and magistrates were not to enforce rights arising from alleged property in another person's person/services as a slave;
  - property acquired by a person could not be denied on the ground that the person was a slave;
  - acts criminal against a free person were equally criminal when done under a pretext of slavery.
- Spatial scope: “Territories of the East India Company,” not an undifferentiated modern India.
- Direction: **supports** a legal non-enforcement/non-recognition breakpoint on 7 April 1843.
- Limit: the text does not state that every enslaved/dependent person was physically emancipated on that date.

### E2 — Andrea Major 2012

Andrea Major, *Slavery, Abolitionism and Empire in India, 1772–1843* (Liverpool University Press, 2012).

- URL/DOI: https://academic.oup.com/liverpool-scholarship-online/book/29317 ; https://doi.org/10.5949/UPO9781846317255
- Class: specialist monograph.
- Relevant propositions:
  - Indian slavery varied strongly by region, social setting and circumstance;
  - colonial actors often represented Indian slavery as qualitatively different from Atlantic slavery;
  - the 1843 measure is best understood as **delegalisation**: courts ceased enforcing slave-property rights, rather than a single act actively freeing every enslaved person.
- Direction: **qualifies** any “slavery ended in 1843” map breakpoint.

### E3 — Anirban Karak 2026

Anirban Karak, “Coerced Labor, Caste, and Aspirations for Liberty in Early Nineteenth-Century Bengal: Revisiting the Antislavery Debate from South Asia,” *Comparative Studies in Society and History*, published online 22 September 2026.

- URL: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/comparative-studies-in-society-and-history/article/coerced-labor-caste-and-aspirations-for-liberty-in-early-nineteenthcentury-bengal-revisiting-the-antislavery-debate-from-south-asia/9B31D4F9611F35A5690D1E6DB630686C
- Class: specialist scholarship.
- Relevant propositions:
  - in Bengal, caste and slavery could operate as overlapping but distinct modes of labor control;
  - Act V ended legal recognition of slavery but did not actively emancipate enslaved people;
  - a narrow legal abolition frame can miss the broader coercive labor relations in which slavery was embedded.
- Direction: **supports** law/practice separation and continued analytical attention after 1843.
- Spatial limit: Bengal evidence is not automatically representative of all Company territories.

### E4 — *Palgrave Handbook* South Asia chapter 2023

“Slavery in South Asia,” *The Palgrave Handbook of Global Slavery throughout History* (2023).

- URL: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-13260-5_29
- Class: specialist synthesis.
- Relevant propositions:
  - describes 1843 as delegalisation rather than complete active emancipation;
  - notes continued slavery after 1843, often under other names/forms;
  - distinguishes this history from later indentured servitude rather than collapsing the systems.
- Direction: **supports** persistence/transition and category separation.

### Counter-pressure checked

The primary law provides a crisp date and legal consequence. Specialist scholarship strongly resists converting that legal date into a clean territorial-practice stop date. Bengal-specific evidence also warns against treating one subregion as the whole Company's territory.

### Frozen baseline proposition

On 7 April 1843 a defensible legal layer can change: Company courts/officers ceased recognizing/enforcing slave-property claims in the specified ways. A territorial-practice layer should **not** flip from slavery-present to slavery-absent on that date. Post-1843 practice requires separate evidence, with regional scope made explicit.

## Q3 — Genoese Black Sea slave-trade network, c. 1260–1500

### E1 — Hannah Barker 2019

Hannah Barker, *That Most Precious Merchandise: The Mediterranean Trade in Black Sea Slaves, 1260–1500* (University of Pennsylvania Press, 2019).

- Publisher URL: https://www.pennpress.org/9781512823660/that-most-precious-merchandise/
- JSTOR record: https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv16t6ckk
- Class: specialist monograph based on Latin/Arabic notarial registers, tax records, law, accounts and other sources.
- Relevant propositions:
  - Genoese, Venetian and Mamluk networks were deeply entangled in Black Sea slave trading;
  - Caffa was a major Genoese-administered hub/export point;
  - state regulation, shipping and commercial infrastructure shaped the trade;
  - Barker separately treats Genoa/Venice/Egypt as “societies with slaves,” not as slave societies.
- Direction: **supports** external/network participation and specific trade-node claims; **does not justify** deriving metropolitan prevalence from route volume.

### E2 — Jeffrey Miner review 2021

Jeffrey Miner, review of Barker, *The Medieval Review* 21.10.16 (2021).

- URL: https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/33443
- Class: independent specialist review/synthesis.
- Relevant propositions:
  - identifies Caffa as the primary export point for Black Sea slaves in Barker's reconstruction;
  - emphasizes Genoese intervention in shipping, taxation and control of ports;
  - notes that the work treats Genoa, Venice and Mamluk Egypt as “societies with slaves,” not economies whose entire structure can be inferred from slave-trade flows.
- Direction: **supports** the network/territory distinction and limits of volume inference.

### E3 — Juliane Schiel 2023

Juliane Schiel, “Slavery in the Western Mediterranean,” in *The Palgrave Handbook of Global Slavery throughout History* (2023), pp. 179–193.

- URL: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-13260-5_10
- Class: independent specialist synthesis, open access.
- Relevant propositions:
  - by about 1400, substantial slave sales occurred in Genoa;
  - enslaved people were present in urban households and other labor settings;
  - source density and labels vary, and household slavery was socially/legal complex rather than a simple all-society condition.
- Direction: **independently supports territorial practice in Genoa city**; this is separate from evidence of Genoese participation in the Black Sea network.
- Spatial limit: city evidence must not silently become a claim about every Ligurian/Genoese-controlled territory.

### E4 — *Palgrave Handbook* Black Sea chapter 2023

“Slavery in the Black Sea Region,” *The Palgrave Handbook of Global Slavery throughout History* (2023).

- URL: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-13260-5_9
- Class: specialist synthesis.
- Relevant bounded example:
  - documents enslaved people being shipped from Caffa toward Genoa, including escape attempts in transit.
- Direction: **supports** route/event-level participation and movement.
- Limit: a voyage/transport event does not by itself establish prevalence at either endpoint.

### E5 — Silvana Fossati Raiteri 2008

Silvana Fossati Raiteri, “I genovesi e il mercato degli schiavi nel Vicino Oriente (secc. XIV–XVI),” *RiMe* 1 (2008).

- URL: https://rime.cnr.it/index.php/rime/article/view/426
- Class: independent specialist article.
- Relevant proposition:
  - describes Genoa as a Mediterranean emporium receiving enslaved people from eastern regions connected to Genoese colonies including Caffa and other nodes.
- Direction: **corroborates** network and Genoa-market connection.

### Temporal/spatial constraint

The book's analytical range continues to 1500, but Genoese Caffa itself ceased to be Genoese after the Ottoman conquest in 1475. A selected-year representation must not extend “Genoese Caffa” to 1500 merely because the book title does.

### Counter-pressure checked

Network evidence is extremely strong and visually tempting. Independent city-level evidence exists for slavery in Genoa, but it comes from different evidence than route/port participation. This case can therefore expose whether an artifact keeps those propositions separate.

### Frozen baseline proposition

Genoese merchants/state structures participated materially in the Black Sea slave trade; Caffa was a major Genoese-administered trade hub until 1475; enslaved people were transported onward to places including Genoa. Separate scholarship also supports household/urban slavery in Genoa itself. The route/network evidence **cannot by itself set territorial-practice prevalence** for Genoa or broader Genoese territories.
