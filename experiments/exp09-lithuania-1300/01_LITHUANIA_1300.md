# Grand Duchy of Lithuania — 1300 CE

Target: `R1:P:1300:D:r1`. Frozen frame: **polity**.  
Research stage: **researched_internal**.  
Research outcome: **BOUNDED_SUPPORTED — unfree household/slavery is supportable in grand-ducal court structures around the 1300 horizon; polity-wide prevalence, uniform status law and later-serfdom continuity are not established**.  
EXP-09 under issue #272; no R1 reviewed-state change, canonical update, P-level,
geometry promotion or publication.

## Bounded synthesis

The strongest target-specific evidence is Artūras Dubonis's synthesis of the developing
grand-ducal court / `valsčius` structure in Lithuania during the second half of the
thirteenth and the fourteenth centuries.

Dubonis states that ruler's courts established in this period settled several categories
of dependent people, including war refugees and **captives (`belaisviai`) identified
within the court's unfree household (`nelaisvoji šeimyna`) / slaves (`vergai`)**.
He separately distinguishes court people as free, partly free and unfree
(`vergai, šeimynykščiai`), and distinguishes those groups from other local people
owing grain, hay, tribute, transport and labor services.

That distinction is methodologically important. Ordinary dues and labor obligations are
not silently converted into slavery. The positive claim rests on the separately named
unfree household/slave category inside the ruler's court economy.

The bounded conclusion is:

> **Around the 1300 horizon, specialist Lithuanian historiography supports the
> presence of an unfree household/slavery category within developing grand-ducal
> court structures in Lithuanian lands. Captives were one documented source of that
> unfree household. This supports a bounded territorial-practice claim for slavery /
> unfree household dependency, but not a claim of uniform practice, prevalence or
> one fixed legal regime across the whole Grand Duchy.**

No P-level is assigned.

## Exact-anchor and reconstruction limit

The evidence is strong for the **second half of the thirteenth through fourteenth
centuries**, a period that directly spans 1300, but it is not an exact-year
administrative register dated 1300.

Dubonis explicitly notes that the reconstruction is supported by concrete historical
sources together with the better-documented fifteenth-century `valsčius` structure and
older analogies. The atlas therefore records a near-anchor institutional presence, not
an invented event on an exact day/year.

The target's polity identity at 1300 was already validated in R1. EXP-09 does not
reopen or strengthen that identity claim.

## Historical categories

The packet preserves the historical / historiographic terms:

- `nelaisvoji šeimyna` — unfree household / familia;
- `vergai` — slaves;
- `šeimynykščiai` — household dependants / members of the unfree household in this
  historiographic context;
- `belaisviai` — captives.

These terms are related but not interchangeable.

The modern Lithuanian encyclopedia's `patriarchaliniai vergai` entry places
patriarchal slaves / `šeimyniškiai` in Lithuania's early-feudal thirteenth-to-late-
fourteenth-century transition into the unfree household. It cites Juozas Jurginis's
1960 study as its historiographic basis. That reference is useful category context,
but the original 1960 article was not recovered in full during this pass and the
encyclopedia is not counted as an independent attestation from its cited scholarship.

The atlas does not force the entire category into a modern universal chattel-slavery
template.

## Captivity is context, not the decisive status inference

Near-anchor chronicles document Lithuanian raiding and civilian capture.

Nicolaus von Jeroschin's fourteenth-century *Chronicle of Prussia*, in Mary Fischer's
modern English translation, describes a Lithuanian raid dated by the translation to
1301–1302 in which girls, women and children taken from Dobrin/Kulmerland were driven
off in chains before being freed by Teutonic forces.

This is strong evidence for near-anchor **capture and forcible movement** by Lithuanian
raiders.

It does not by itself establish:
- the captives' final legal status in Lithuania;
- that every captive became a slave;
- the prevalence of slaveholding inside Lithuanian territory.

Jeroschin also substantially depends on Peter of Dusburg's chronicle tradition. It is
therefore one chronicle family, not an independent second narrative witness.

## Specialist captivity control

Alvydas Nikžentaitis's study of captives in Lithuania and the Teutonic Order state,
1283–1409, is a critical boundary control.

Its synthesis shows that captive treatment changed over time:
- early in the conflict, lower-ranking captives were often killed rather than retained
  as economically valuable prisoners;
- regular ransom/exchange and broader economic exploitation became especially visible
  from the second half of the fourteenth century;
- later captives could be settled, sold or put to work.

This prevents two bad inferences:

1. **capture = slave status automatically**;
2. later fourteenth-/fifteenth-century captive economics = exact 1300 practice.

Nikžentaitis therefore narrows the raid evidence while remaining compatible with
Dubonis's separate institutional evidence for an unfree household in the ruler's
court system.

## Later serfdom and statutes are excluded from the anchor claim

Later Grand Duchy evidence is much denser.

Neringa Dambrauskaitė's research on sixteenth- and early-seventeenth-century noble
households documents `familia illibera` / unfree people and the 1529 Statute's
formal categories of entry into unfreedom.

That material is valuable as a **negative temporal control**:
it shows why later legal precision must not be projected backward to 1300.

EXP-09 does not claim that:
- the 1529 categories already existed unchanged in 1300;
- later serfdom was already fully formed in 1300;
- every later `šeimynykštis`, serf or dependent maps directly onto the earlier
  `nelaisvoji šeimyna`.

## Labor obligations are not slavery

Dubonis separately describes local `valsčius` people providing:
- grain and hay;
- honey and fur tribute;
- hospitality / provisioning;
- castle, road and bridge labor;
- transport obligations;
- collective labor.

Those obligations are evidence of dependency and extraction.

They are **not** used as proof of slavery.

The positive slavery/unfree-household interpretation comes from the distinct court
population explicitly categorized as unfree (`vergai, šeimynykščiai`).

## Territorial and social scope

Evidence locus:
- developing grand-ducal court / domain structures in Lithuanian lands in the late
  thirteenth–fourteenth centuries;
- near-anchor Lithuanian raiding/capture outside the polity as a separate external
  mechanism.

Dubonis also emphasizes uneven direct ducal control: court/domain structures were not
uniformly distributed, and direct grand-ducal authority was especially different in
Samogitia and other areas.

Maximum defensible inference:

> **Slavery / unfree-household dependency was institutionally present in at least part
> of the grand-ducal court economy around 1300.**

Required abstention:
- no whole-polity prevalence estimate;
- no uniform institution across Lithuanian, Samogitian and Ruthenian territories;
- no assumption every court had the same composition;
- no exact statutory slave law at 1300;
- no captive-to-slave conversion without status evidence;
- no later-serfdom back-projection;
- no practice polygon.

## Geometry state

**Polity identity valid; practice geometry unresolved.**

The changing political extent of the Grand Duchy does not define the extent of the
unfree-household institution. No slavery/practice polygon is authorized.

## Source dependency and access audit

- Baronas–Dubonis–Petrauskas 2011 is the principal modern specialist synthesis.
  The Orbis Lituaniae page reproduces Dubonis's bounded section and names the academic
  volume as its source. The full monograph was not exhaustively reread in this pass.
- VLE's `patriarchaliniai vergai` entry is reference synthesis and explicitly cites
  Jurginis 1960. The original Jurginis article was bibliographically recovered but not
  read in full; do not count the encyclopedia and Jurginis as independent evidence.
- Jeroschin/Fischer is a near-anchor primary narrative via modern translation, but the
  relevant passage was recovered through a searchable mirror while publisher and
  institutional metadata verified the edition. Jeroschin's chronicle substantially
  follows Peter of Dusburg and belongs to the same chronicle chain.
- Nikžentaitis 1997/1999 is specialist captivity synthesis and acts primarily as a
  temporal/category constraint.
- Dambrauskaitė 2019 is deliberately later comparison/control only.

No claim of exhaustive Lithuanian, Latin, Ruthenian, German or Polish archival search
is made.

## Research disposition

**BOUNDED_SUPPORTED.**

Supported:
- an unfree household/slave category in grand-ducal court structures during a period
  directly spanning the 1300 anchor;
- captives as one source of that unfree household in the specialist reconstruction;
- near-anchor Lithuanian capture/forced movement in raiding warfare.

Not supported:
- polity-wide prevalence or scale;
- a uniform legal regime across the Grand Duchy;
- universal household slaveholding;
- all captives becoming slaves;
- exact 1300 statutory codification;
- automatic equivalence between unfree household, serfdom and modern chattel slavery.

## Adversarial summary

- **SURVIVES:** a bounded positive around 1300 because the strongest specialist source
  directly places `nelaisvoji šeimyna / vergai` inside late-13th/14th-century
  grand-ducal court structures.
- **REVISE / NARROW:** describe it as period-spanning reconstructed institutional
  presence, not an exact-year administrative event.
- **REJECT:** Lithuanian raiding alone proves slave status.
- **REJECT:** ordinary tribute/labor obligations prove slavery.
- **REJECT:** sixteenth-century statutes or mature serfdom can be back-projected.
- **REJECT:** one institutional locus establishes polity-wide prevalence.
- **DEPENDENCY CONTROL:** Jeroschin/Peter of Dusburg is one chronicle family; VLE /
  Jurginis is one historiographic lineage unless the original study is separately
  reconstructed.
