# Temple of Amun at Karnak — 1000 BCE

Target: `R1:N:karnak_amun_m1000`. Frozen frame: **institution_estate**.  
Research stage: **researched_internal**.  
Research outcome: **RESEARCHED_INCONCLUSIVE — earlier New Kingdom Amun-temple captive/enslaved labour is well supported and near-anchor Amun-linked unfree/service/property relations are documented, but Temple-of-Amun slave/unfree labour at or around 1000 BCE is not securely established**.  
EXP-11 under issue #276; no R1 reviewed-state change, canonical update, P-level,
geometry promotion, cumulative-candidate integration or publication.

## Bounded synthesis

The research produces a deliberately asymmetric result.

There is strong **target-specific earlier evidence** that the Temple of Amun-Ra at
Karnak received prisoners of war and used captive/enslaved labour during the New
Kingdom. Specialist work on New Kingdom prisoners and textile production ties
captives and enslaved workers to Amun's temple workshops and to royal allocations
recorded in sources such as Papyrus Harris I.

The 1000 BCE anchor, however, falls after the New Kingdom, in the early Third
Intermediate Period. The near-anchor evidence recovered for the Amun institution is
different in character:

- the Twenty-first-Dynasty Banishment Stela uses `bꜣk.w` for people associated with
  Amun/Karnak, but specialist editions interpret the context as quarrelsome
  servants/priests or political exiles, not a slave-property transaction;
- a Menkheperre stela describes an Asiatic domestic population inside/encroaching on
  the Great Amun temple enclosure, but the source does not establish their slave,
  servile or compulsory-labour status;
- Amun-priestly property decrees around the same period document purchases of land,
  people/services and, in one case, services purchased in perpetuity, but the clearest
  labour-service transaction concerns personnel of the Temple of Osiris at Abydos and
  elite family property. It is not evidence that those people laboured as slaves
  inside the Temple of Amun at Karnak.

Broader specialist synthesis confirms that slavery and coerced labour existed in
Third Intermediate Period Egypt, but stresses sparse documentation, changing social
conditions and unstable terminology.

The maximum defensible conclusion is therefore:

> **The Temple of Amun at Karnak had a securely attested history of captive/enslaved
> labour in the New Kingdom and remained a powerful institution around 1000 BCE.
> Near the anchor, Amun-linked texts document subordinate persons, population inside
> the temple enclosure and perpetual service/property transactions, but the reviewed
> evidence does not securely identify a slave or equivalent unfree-labour population
> working for the Temple of Amun itself around 1000 BCE. The selected-anchor
> slavery/unfree-labour state remains researched-inconclusive.**

No P-level is assigned.

## Exact-anchor institutional context

EXP-10 already qualified the Temple/Precinct of Amun as a valid institutional locus at
1000 BCE.

Twenty-first-Dynasty evidence keeps that institution firmly in view. Menkheperre was
High Priest of Amun through much of the eleventh century BCE and the Amun priesthood
held substantial political and economic authority in Thebes. Archaeological and
epigraphic work places Twenty-first-Dynasty settlement and construction in/around
Karnak.

This institutional continuity is **not** labour-status continuity.

## Earlier New Kingdom positive evidence

Marta Valerio's specialist synthesis of New Kingdom prisoners identifies temples as
major recipients of prisoners used as labour, with the Temple of Amun-Ra at Karnak a
particularly important beneficiary. Royal war booty included people as well as goods.

Jordan Galczynski's study of New Kingdom textile production similarly treats captives
and enslaved people as labourers in state/temple production. It discusses `mrt` and
other status terms, including captives assigned to Amun's textile workshops.

This evidence establishes a real target-specific precedent:
**Amun/Karnak used captive/enslaved labour in the New Kingdom.**

It does not establish persistence to 1000 BCE.

Papyrus Harris I belongs to the reign of Ramesses III, well before the selected
anchor. The New Kingdom itself ends conventionally around 1069 BCE, and the transition
into the Third Intermediate Period materially changed military acquisition, political
organization and documentation.

## Third Intermediate Period category control

Ella Karev's recent synthesis emphasizes that slavery and coerced labour changed
substantially over Egyptian history and that the Third Intermediate Period has
especially sparse documentation. Her overview records slave sales in the period,
including transactions involving groups of people, but those examples are not a
Karnak/Amun-temple labour register.

Antonio Loprieno likewise warns against treating Egyptian terms such as `ḥm` and
`bꜣk` as stable equivalents of modern "slave." His synthesis notes that during the
first millennium BCE explicit slavery references become rarer while other servitude
relationships become more visible.

These sources block the inference:

> New Kingdom Amun captive labour + continued Amun institution = slave labour at
> Karnak in 1000 BCE.

## The Banishment Stela: `bꜣk.w` is not enough

The Twenty-first-Dynasty Banishment Stela is highly relevant because it belongs to
Menkheperre's Amun/Karnak political world and uses `bꜣk.w`.

The Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae identifies the text and its specialist editions.
Modern discussion of the inscription renders the group as quarrelsome servants, and
some scholarship describes them more specifically as servants/priests of the Karnak
temple. They had been banished to an oasis after political conflict and were later
recalled.

The episode establishes:
- Amun-oracular authority over punishment/amnesty;
- subordinated/serving persons described with `bꜣk.w`;
- a coercive political sanction (banishment).

It does **not** securely establish:
- ownership of those people as slaves;
- purchase/sale;
- hereditary status;
- compulsory productive labour;
- a temple slave workforce.

Given the semantic range of `bꜣk`, translating this occurrence directly as "slave"
would overstate the evidence.

## The Asiatic domestic population at Karnak

James Edward Bennett's archaeological synthesis notes that a Twenty-first-Dynasty
stela of Menkheperre describes an **Asiatic domestic population** encroaching into the
walls of the Great Amun temple at Karnak.

This is valuable because it is both target-specific and near-anchor.

But the status is unresolved. The source does not establish that these people were:
- captives;
- owned;
- unfree;
- temple labourers;
- hereditary dependants.

Ethnic/foreign identity must not substitute for status.

The population therefore remains contextual evidence only.

## Amun-priestly property and perpetual services

Brian Muhs's economic history of the Third Intermediate Period gives the strongest
near-anchor evidence for formally alienable service relations in the Amun-priestly
world.

Menkheperre's oracular property settlement, inscribed in the Khonsu temple at Karnak,
uses Amun-Re to confirm purchases and ownership of fields.

A later Twenty-first-Dynasty settlement involving members of the High-Priest-of-Amun
family records property purchases and the purchase of the **services** of specialist
workers from the Temple of Osiris at Abydos. Muhs interprets those services as being
purchased in perpetuity; other people in the property package may have been acquired
together with land/cattle they tended.

This supports:
- near-anchor legal/economic capacity to alienate long-term personal services;
- an Amun-priestly elite property environment that included highly subordinate
  service relations.

It does not establish:
- that the workers belonged to the Temple of Amun;
- that the service took place at Karnak;
- that every person in these transactions was legally a slave;
- that the Temple of Amun itself owned/used them as a labour force.

The evidence locus and institutional owner must remain separate.

## Shabti language is not human slavery evidence

Near-anchor funerary decrees for the Amun high-priestly family sometimes use
sale/service language for shabti figurines.

Those objects symbolically perform labour for the deceased.

They are not human labourers and are excluded from the historical-practice claim.

This is recorded because the terminology can otherwise generate a false positive in
keyword-driven research.

## Law / practice

No exact-1000 BCE Karnak labour register, slave-sale record, manumission record or
personnel list was recovered that securely classifies a human Temple-of-Amun worker as
a slave or equivalent unfree property status.

Third Intermediate Period Egyptian slavery exists in broader specialist synthesis.
Amun-priestly property/service transactions also exist.

Neither automatically establishes Temple-of-Amun practice.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci:
- Temple/Precinct of Amun at Karnak, New Kingdom captive/enslaved labour;
- Great Amun enclosure and Amun oracular texts, Twenty-first Dynasty;
- Amun high-priestly family property transactions at/through Karnak;
- wider Third Intermediate Period Egyptian slavery/servitude context.

Maximum defensible inference:

**Earlier Amun/Karnak captive/enslaved labour is supported; around 1000 BCE the
institution remains powerful and is embedded in a world containing alienable,
perpetual and coercive service relations, but target-specific Temple-of-Amun
slave/unfree labour at the anchor is not securely demonstrated.**

Required abstention:
- no continuity inference from Ramesses III/New Kingdom to 1000 BCE;
- no `bꜣk` = slave mapping by default;
- no Asiatic/foreign = slave mapping;
- no Amun-priestly household/property = Temple-of-Amun labour mapping;
- no shabti "slave" language as human practice;
- no prevalence/scale estimate;
- no practice polygon.

## Geometry state

No practice polygon is authorized.

## Source dependency and access audit

- Karev 2023 and Loprieno 2012 are broad specialist category/period controls; they are
  independent syntheses but overlap in the wider Egyptological slavery literature.
- Valerio 2023 and Galczynski 2024 independently synthesize New Kingdom labour, but
  materially overlap in the primary/source tradition for Amun-temple captives and
  royal allocations; they are not counted as two independent near-anchor attestations.
- Bennett 2019 provides target-specific Third Intermediate Period settlement evidence;
  it does not supply a status classification for the Asiatic domestic population.
- The Banishment Stela is one primary textual family; translations/editions are not
  counted as independent sources.
- Muhs 2016 supplies near-anchor property/service interpretation. The clearest
  perpetual-service passage concerns personnel connected to Abydos in an Amun-priestly
  family settlement and is kept outside a direct Karnak labour claim.
- No exhaustive search of unpublished Demotic/hieratic material, Karnak priestly
  archives or all specialist non-English literature is claimed.

## Research disposition

**RESEARCHED_INCONCLUSIVE for Temple-of-Amun slavery/unfree labour around 1000 BCE.**

Preserve as supported context:
- New Kingdom Amun/Karnak captive/enslaved labour;
- Third Intermediate Period slavery/alienable persons in Egypt more broadly;
- near-anchor Amun-priestly property relations involving perpetual services;
- near-anchor subordinate persons and an Asiatic domestic population in the Amun
  institutional environment.

Do not promote as supported:
- a Temple-of-Amun slave workforce at 1000 BCE;
- persistence of Ramesside captive-labour arrangements into the Twenty-first Dynasty;
- slave status for `bꜣk.w` on the Banishment Stela;
- slave status for the Asiatic domestic population;
- temple-wide prevalence or estate extent.

Reopen/deepen if:
- a Twenty-first-Dynasty Karnak personnel, property, sale, dedication or labour text
  securely identifies human slave/unfree status tied to the Temple of Amun itself;
- specialist scholarship demonstrates continuity of a specific Amun captive/slave
  labour institution across the New Kingdom–Third Intermediate transition;
- a source-critical study resolves the status of the Menkheperre-era domestic
  population or `bꜣk.w` in a way consequential to labour status.

## Adversarial findings

- **SUPPORTED EARLIER PERIOD:** New Kingdom Temple of Amun captive/enslaved labour.
- **SUPPORTED BROADER PERIOD:** slavery and alienable service relations existed in
  Third Intermediate Period Egypt.
- **SUPPORTED NEAR-ANCHOR CONTEXT:** Amun-linked property decrees could alienate
  long-term/perpetual services.
- **REJECT:** New Kingdom target-specific evidence proves 1000 BCE continuity.
- **REJECT:** `bꜣk.w` on the Banishment Stela automatically means slaves.
- **REJECT:** Asiatic domestic population automatically means captive/slave labour.
- **REJECT:** Amun high-priestly private/family property relations equal Temple-of-Amun
  institutional labour.
- **UNRESOLVED:** human slave/unfree labour specifically inside the Amun institution at
  the selected anchor.
- **PORTABLE CONTRACT SURVIVES:** the existing temporal, category, locus and
  institution/owner distinctions express the result without a schema extension.
