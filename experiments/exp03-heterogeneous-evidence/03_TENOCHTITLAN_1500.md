# EXP-03 case 3 — Tenochtitlan, c. 1500 CE

**State:** experimental research packet
**Target type:** altepetl / imperial capital within the Mexico-Tenochtitlan–Tlatelolco urban complex
**Anchor:** c. 1500 CE
**Research outcome:** bounded_supported_with_category_boundary
**Canonical effect:** none

## Bounded proposition

Recent specialist Nahua scholarship supports the existence of the category *tlacotli* / plural *tlatlacotin* in Mexica society and documents marketable subcategories, exchange in markets including Tlatelolco, and traders associated with Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco.

This is sufficient for a bounded proposition that a servile/slave category conventionally translated as “slave” was institutionally present in the Mexica capital context around the conquest-era period. The category must not be treated as automatically equivalent to Atlantic chattel slavery or as internally uniform.

## Required abstention

Do not:
- translate every occurrence of *tlacotli* into a universal modern slavery category without a category note;
- infer prevalence from the scale or fame of the Tlatelolco market;
- use post-conquest Spanish descriptions as if they were unmediated pre-conquest administrative records;
- infer the entire Mexica imperial territory from evidence in the capital-region market and social system;
- merge Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco into one spatial identity merely because their urban histories are tightly connected.

## Evidence locus / inference extent

**Evidence locus:** Mexica/Nahua society, Tlatelolco market, and Tenochtitlan/Tlatelolco merchant context.
**Maximum defensible inference:** the *tlacotli* category and trade in some *tlatlacotin* were part of the capital-region social/economic system close to the c. 1500 anchor.

## Practice / status terminology

Historical term: **tlacotli / tlatlacotin**.

Specialist work shows multiple associated terms and subcategories. Spanish colonial lexicographers and chroniclers commonly rendered these terms with “slave/servant” vocabulary, but that translation is an analytical mapping rather than identity of institutions.

**Mapping status:** approximate / historically specific; requires explicit terminology note.

## Law / practice

The present packet is primarily social/economic/status evidence. It does not infer a complete legal regime from market descriptions.

## Network / territorial

Tlatelolco market connections and long-distance merchants demonstrate exchange networks. Those networks do not prove territorial practice at every connected market or origin.

## Geometry state

**historical_place_resolved; Tenochtitlan_and_Tlatelolco_distinct; urban_extent_unreviewed**

The historical capital location is known, but the experiment does not select a c. 1500 polygon. Tlatelolco evidence remains explicitly localized rather than silently absorbed into a Tenochtitlan polygon.

## Contrary / limiting evidence

The strongest limitation is source mediation: much of the detailed written evidence is sixteenth-/seventeenth-century and filtered through colonial-era recording and translation. That makes **source role and temporal relationship to the anchor** material to interpretation.

## Access / language limitation

This pass used Spanish-language specialist scholarship but did not independently collate Nahuatl manuscripts or all colonial source editions. Translation choices remain a first-class uncertainty.

## Coverage confidence

**Medium-high for category/institution presence; low for prevalence; medium for exact city-specific extent; high that category translation requires an explicit warning.**

## Portability pressure

This case falsifies any portable core that lacks a structured way to preserve **historical terminology / category-mapping status**. Hiding *tlacotli* only inside prose would make later views prone to false equivalence.
