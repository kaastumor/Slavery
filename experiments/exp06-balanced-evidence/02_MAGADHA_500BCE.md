# Magadha – Haryanka dynasty — 500 BCE

Research stage: **under_review**.
Research outcome: **bounded_supported_mid_first_millennium_slavery_context_exact_anchor_dynasty_uncertain**.

## Bounded synthesis

The evidence for slavery and slave/servant status in the social world associated with
early Magadha is materially stronger than the exact chronology of the frozen target.

Specialist synthesis of the middle Ganga valley in the mid-first millennium BCE treats
slavery as one component of the social transition accompanying state formation and the
rise of monarchies including Magadha. Early Buddhist textual traditions framed around
Magadhan kings Bimbisāra and Ajātasattu also assume masters' claims over dāsa persons,
runaway slaves, royal household slave/workmen and social distinctions between free and
unfree status.

The exact statement “Haryanka Magadha at 500 BCE” is less secure. Absolute chronology
for the historical Buddha and the kings tied to his life is disputed. Older long
chronologies can place Bimbisāra immediately before and Ajātasattu immediately after
500 BCE; shorter chronologies shift both rulers substantially later. There is no
contemporary 500 BCE inscription naming a Haryanka dynasty and simultaneously
documenting slave status.

The correct result is therefore two-layered:

- **mid-first-millennium Magadhan/monarchical slavery context: bounded-supported**;
- **exact Haryanka dynastic observation at 500 BCE: under review because of chronology
  and textual-transmission limits**.

No P-level is assigned.

## Frame and chronology

“Haryanka dynasty” is a conventional historical reconstruction based principally on
later Buddhist, Jain and Purāṇic traditions.

The chronology depends heavily on how the Buddha's lifetime and death are dated.
Modern scholarship does not supply one uncontested absolute chronology. Choong's 2004
review lists substantially different modern datings for the Buddha, while Sarao's 2003
study is part of the shorter-chronology debate.

Consequently:
- the anchor 500 BCE cannot be treated as an exact regnal cross-section;
- a Bimbisāra or Ajātasattu narrative is evidence for the early Magadhan social world,
  not proof that the described event occurred exactly in 500 BCE;
- the frozen dynastic label is retained for the experiment but the temporal fit remains
  explicitly approximate/contested.

Unlike the Chámpa case, the issue is not that Magadha is an invented geographic frame.
The problem is the exact dynastic/royal chronology attached to the anchor.

## Vinaya Mahāvagga I.34 — Dāsavatthu

The Pāli Vinaya presents a slave who runs away and enters the Buddhist order. His
masters identify him as “our dāsa” and propose taking him back. Others invoke King
Seniya Bimbisāra of Magadha's protection for those who have gone forth. The resulting
monastic rule states that a dāsa should not be ordained.

This is important because the narrative presupposes:
- recognizable master–slave claims;
- fugitive status;
- the possibility of conflict between a master's claim and monastic status;
- Magadhan royal authority as part of the story world.

It is not a contemporary royal decree copied from Bimbisāra's chancery. The Vinaya
tradition was transmitted and redacted after the period it describes. It therefore
supports a bounded social/legal-status tradition associated with early Magadha, not an
exact 500 BCE legislative act.

## Sāmaññaphala Sutta — Ajātasattu's slave/workman

The Dīgha Nikāya's Sāmaññaphala Sutta addresses King Ajātasattu of Magadha and asks
him to imagine a man in his household who is his “slave, his workman,” rising before
him, retiring after him and acting at his command, then leaving household life for the
religious life.

The thought experiment assumes that a Magadhan king could intelligibly be represented
as possessing a dāsa/kammakāra whose service was highly subordinated.

Again, this is a literary-religious dialogue, not a contemporary payroll or sale
contract. Its value is the social relation it assumes, not exact-year quantification.

## Specialist synthesis: Dev Raj and Romila Thapar

Dev Raj's 1957 study of slavery in Pāli and Sanskrit sources treats the Buddhist-period
monarchies as societies in which slaves could appear in domestic, agricultural,
commercial and military service. He also emphasizes multiple routes into slavery,
including war, debt and birth.

That synthesis is valuable but must be source-critically bounded:
- many examples derive from narrative and commentarial layers of different dates;
- later commentaries can sharpen categories not securely recoverable from the earliest
  textual layer;
- “dāsa-kammakāra” combines categories whose exact legal/economic relationship should
  not be flattened automatically.

Romila Thapar's work on the mid-first-millennium Ganga valley independently places
slavery within the broader transition to state society and the emergence of monarchies
such as Kosala and Magadha. Her analysis also argues against treating slavery as the
single dominant labor relation: hired labor, peasants, household dependency, caste/
status hierarchy and other forms coexisted.

This is the correct scale for the anchor: a supported institutional/social context,
not a slave-population percentage.

## Category controls

Relevant categories include:

- **dāsa / dāsī** — often slave in the early Buddhist social vocabulary, but translation
  must remain source-specific;
- **kammakāra** — worker/laborer, not automatically slave;
- **dāsa-kammakāra** — compound social/labor category that should not be read as proof
  that every worker was enslaved;
- **bhataka / bhṛtaka** — hired/paid labor categories, distinct from slave status;
- **ārāmika** and later monastic service categories — require chronological and
  institutional care and cannot be back-projected wholesale into early Magadha;
- captives, debt-bondsmen and household-born slaves — distinct acquisition pathways,
  not one homogeneous status.

The packet therefore rejects “dependent labor = slavery” and rejects a universal
translation of every service term as dāsa-status.

## Law / practice

No contemporary Haryanka slave code or sale archive was recovered.

The Vinaya case encodes a social/legal conflict over a runaway dāsa and monastic
ordination. The Sāmaññaphala passage encodes royal household dependency. These are
practice/status traditions, not a complete state law code.

Later Dharmaśāstra or Arthaśāstra classifications are not back-projected to 500 BCE
Magadha as if they were Haryanka law.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence locus:
- early Magadhan royal settings in the Buddhist narrative tradition;
- the wider middle Ganga valley social formation discussed by specialist scholarship;
- Rājagaha/Rājagṛha as the principal royal/monastic narrative setting.

Maximum defensible inference:

**Slavery and slave-status relations were part of the social world represented for
early Magadhan monarchy and the mid-first-millennium Ganga valley, but the exact
placement of the Haryanka dynasty and its named rulers at 500 BCE is chronologically
contested and cannot be presented as an exact cross-section.**

Required abstention:
- no exact 500 BCE Bimbisāra/Ajātasattu event unless chronology is independently fixed;
- no population/prevalence estimate from narrative frequency;
- no equation of dāsa, kammakāra, bhataka and other dependants;
- no Haryanka legal code inferred from Buddhist monastic literature;
- no later Mauryan/Kauṭilyan law back-projected to the anchor.

## Geometry state

**historical polity extent unresolved for the exact anchor.**

Rājagaha is a defensible reference locus. A modern Bihar polygon or later Magadhan/
Mauryan extent is not an acceptable 500 BCE practice polygon.

## Source dependency and access audit

- Mahāvagga I.34 is one Vinaya textual tradition; modern translations and Dev Raj's
  discussion of the same episode are dependent on that source.
- The Sāmaññaphala Sutta is a separate Pāli textual tradition, but still part of the
  broader transmitted Buddhist corpus rather than a contemporary archive.
- Dev Raj synthesizes many Pāli/Sanskrit sources and later commentaries; source count
  inside that synthesis is not independent corroboration.
- Thapar supplies a separate modern social-formation interpretation of the middle
  Ganga evidence base.
- chronology studies such as Sarao/Choong concern the dating framework, not slavery
  attestations.

Pāli text and modern English/French specialist scholarship were consulted. No
independent manuscript recension, Sanskrit/Jain parallel tradition or philological
dating of individual canonical strata was performed.

## Reopening / resolution condition

The slavery-context result does not need more source volume.

Reopen the exact target-time classification if a future historical chronology review
can establish a defensible absolute range for the intended Haryanka phase around the
500 BCE anchor, or if a securely dated contemporary/near-contemporary Magadhan source
links slave/status evidence to that phase.

Until then the target remains under review for exact anchor fit, not for historical
absence.

## Adversarial findings

- **REJECT:** one traditional Bimbisāra/Ajātasattu chronology is exact fact.
- **REJECT:** the Vinaya episode is a verbatim Haryanka royal statute.
- **REJECT:** dāsa-kammakāra means every worker was a slave.
- **REJECT:** frequent slaves in Buddhist narrative permit a prevalence estimate.
- **REJECT:** later Mauryan/Dharmaśāstra rules can be back-projected to 500 BCE.
- **SUPPORTED, bounded:** slavery/slave-status relations belong in the social history
  of early Magadha and the mid-first-millennium Ganga valley.
- **UNDER REVIEW:** exact “Haryanka dynasty at 500 BCE” placement.
- **PORTABLE CONTRACT SURVIVES:** positive period-level social evidence and contested
  exact chronology can coexist without schema extension.
