# Mongol Yam/postal network — 1300 CE

## Bounded synthesis

The Mongol yam around 1300 is a strong positive **coerced-labour network** case, but it
is not a slave-status case by default.

Ögödei's institutionalization of the relay system assigned people, horses, provisions
and station personnel to maintain fixed post stations. Later Yuan and Tibetan evidence
shows special relay households carrying compulsory transport/service obligations.
Around the exact 1300 anchor, Ghazan's Ilkhanid reform sought to curb abusive and
arbitrary exactions connected with the same wider Mongol relay institution.

This supports a bounded conclusion:

**the Mongol relay network depended on structured compulsory labour and material
obligations imposed on designated households and communities across multiple regional
branches around the thirteenth/fourteenth-century transition.**

Specialist scholarship independently documents slavery, war captivity and forced
migration throughout Mongol Eurasia. Those institutions are historically related to
Mongol mobilization but are not evidence that every jamuchin/zhanhu/postal household
was enslaved.

No territorial P-level is assigned to the mobile network.

## Network frame and chronology

The frozen target is explicitly a **mobile network**, which fits the evidence better
than a polity polygon.

The institutional foundation predates the anchor. The Secret History of the Mongols
attributes systematic post-station organization to Ögödei, with relay personnel,
horses and supplies assigned from imperial administrative units. The purpose included
stopping messengers from taking resources indiscriminately from settlements.

The system clearly continued through the late thirteenth and early fourteenth
centuries: Yuan administration maintained special postal households; Tibet had a
Mongol-Sakya station network based on compulsory household service; and Ghazan,
ruling the Ilkhanate from 1295 to 1304, reformed yam financing and authorized use
across the 1300 anchor.

The temporal result is therefore **network-period support with exact-anchor continuity
and reform evidence**, rather than a 1300 claim manufactured from one older decree.

## Ögödei's institutional foundation

In the Secret History, Ögödei states that imperial messengers riding freely through
settlements caused suffering. The solution was to establish post stations and assign
post-station and post-horse keepers.

The passage describes households/administrative units supplying animals and personnel,
assigned herders and storekeepers, and roughly twenty post-horse keepers per station.

This is source-native evidence for a state-created service obligation. It does not
show chattel slave status for the workers.

## Jamuchin / zhanhu relay households

Specialist synthesis in The Mongol World describes relay-station households as a
distinct Mongol imperial institution: Mongolian jamuchin and Chinese zhanhu.

In steppe regions, each minggan was to maintain a station **in lieu of other ordinary
taxation and corvée**. In North China, station and civilian households jointly supplied
needed goods. Brose's work on Yuan household classification likewise treats zhanhu as
a special service-household category designed to extract labor/resources for state
functions.

This supports a coerced-service reading. It does not justify station household = slave.

## Yuan administrative practice: burden, flight and replacement

Yuan administrative material records practical problems with relay service, including
station households that fled or became insufficient and the need to replenish or
reassign service households.

That matters because it moves beyond a normative decree: the relay obligation had
material consequences in practice. The official-history compilation still cannot
quantify lived burden everywhere.

## Central Tibet: ulag and compulsory station service

Specialist work on Mongol-Sakya Tibet describes ulag / 'u lag as postal/transport
service and identifies it as one of the heaviest obligations imposed on Tibetan
households.

The evidence includes compulsory labor, provision of horses and food, household
impoverishment, households abandoning stations because the burden was too heavy, and
large station-support assignments involving thousands of households.

This is strong regional practice evidence for coerced relay labor. It remains
region-specific and cannot define every Yuan, Ilkhanid, Chagataid or Jochid branch.

## Ghazan's reform around 1300

Ghazan's reign, 1295–1304, supplies the strongest exact-anchor evidence.

Kim's specialist analysis explains that Ghazan restricted yam use to authorized
envoys carrying valid paiza, curbed royal/high-official misuse, ended arbitrary local
collection of relay expenses and shifted necessary expenses toward central budgeting.

The reform itself is evidence that relay exactions were a live governance problem.
It does not mean compulsory relay labor vanished across all Mongol domains.

## Broader Mongol slavery is a separate institution

Biran's specialist chapter documents extensive slavery, forced migration and captive
redistribution across the Mongol Empire, including war captives, slave markets and a
spectrum of dependency from free people to full slaves.

This confirms that slavery was part of the wider Mongol imperial social world. It
does not authorize relabeling relay households as slaves. The yam can be a coerced-
labour system while its designated laborers remained legally/socially distinct from
enslaved captives.

## Category controls

Relevant categories:
- yam / jam — relay/postal network;
- jamuchin / zhanhu — designated relay-station households;
- ulag / 'u lag — postal/transport horse and labor obligation in Tibetan usage;
- paiza — authorization credential for official travel/use of relay facilities;
- corvée/tax-service obligations attached to households;
- slaves, war captives and forced migrants in the broader Mongol imperial system.

Required mapping:
- station household is not automatically slave;
- compulsory corvée/transport service is not automatically chattel slavery;
- imperial forced migration is not yam service;
- enslaved captive is not ordinary zhanhu/jamuchin by default.

## Law / practice

The evidence includes both normative structure and implementation.

Normative/administrative structure includes Ögödei's station reform, Yuan regulations
and Ghazan's reform. Practice evidence includes station-household flight/replacement,
Tibetan impoverishment and compulsory service, and reports of arbitrary exactions.

## Network / territory

The positive historical object is **the relay network and its regional service
branches**.

Do not color every Mongol-controlled territory as having an identical relay burden,
infer that every settlement crossed by a yam route supplied coerced labor, or transfer
broader Mongol slavery prevalence into the yam.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence loci include the imperial Mongol/Ögödei institutional tradition, Yuan China
station-household administration, Central Tibetan Mongol-Sakya relay practice and
Ilkhanid Iran under Ghazan.

Maximum defensible inference:

**around 1300, the wider Mongol yam was maintained through structured compulsory
station-household labor and resource obligations in multiple regional branches, with
documented burdens and reform efforts. This is a positive coerced-labour network
result, separate from the Mongol Empire's independently documented systems of slavery
and forced migration.**

Required abstention:
- no station-household = slave normalization;
- no network-wide uniform burden estimate;
- no slave prevalence inferred from station counts;
- no territorial practice polygon;
- no assumption that every Mongol captive/forced migrant worked in the yam;
- no assumption that Ghazan's reform applied unchanged to Yuan/Tibetan branches.

## Source dependency and access audit

- Secret History translations are one primary textual chain; specialist histories
  quoting the same station decree are not independent foundation events.
- The Mongol World chapter and Brose synthesize overlapping Yuan household evidence;
  they are interpretations, not multiple station-household events.
- Yuan shi administrative notices form an official-history compilation and should not
  be multiplied by modern retellings.
- Tibetan postal scholarship uses Chinese/Tibetan records and supplies a distinct
  regional practice locus within the same imperial institution.
- Kim's Ghazan study supplies a distinct Ilkhanid branch and exact-anchor reform.
- Biran's slavery synthesis is category context, not yam-labor evidence.

English translations and specialist scholarship plus accessible Chinese/Tibetan
administrative summaries were reviewed. No independent Mongolian, Chinese, Persian or
Tibetan critical edition was produced.

## Adversarial findings

- **REJECT:** zhanhu/jamuchin station households were therefore slaves.
- **REJECT:** Mongol slavery evidence proves the yam was staffed by slaves.
- **REJECT:** station/personnel counts can become coerced-labor prevalence.
- **REJECT:** one regional branch proves identical burden throughout Mongol Eurasia.
- **SUPPORTED:** compulsory relay labor/resource obligations were intrinsic to the
  network in multiple regional branches.
- **SUPPORTED:** Ghazan's reform supplies exact-anchor evidence that relay exactions
  were a live governance problem around 1300.
- **PORTABLE CONTRACT SURVIVES:** network coerced labor and separate slavery systems
  coexist without category collapse or schema extension.
