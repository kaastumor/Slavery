# Later Mayan City-States — 1300 CE

Experimental packet, 2026-09-24; no R1 reviewed-state change, canonical update, P-level,

## Bounded synthesis

The frozen target is an aggregate, not one Maya state.

At the 1300 CE anchor, Mayapán in northern Yucatán provides a strong in-period locus.
Archaeological and chronological research places the city's main demographic expansion
through the thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, and retrospective Maya/colonial
historical traditions place a period of political violence in 1302–1323.

Specialist synthesis of Mayapán describes warfare in which captives could be taken for
sale into slavery or for sacrifice, alongside required military service/corvée-like
obligations. Archaeology independently supports intensive warfare, captive-taking,
violent trauma and sacrifice, but those material indicators do not by themselves assign
slave legal status.

This supports a bounded conclusion:

**slaving/captive-sale practices are historically supported for the Mayapán political
system in the Postclassic period around the 1300 anchor, but that one locus cannot be
converted into a uniform classification for all Later Maya city-states.**

No aggregate-wide P-level or practice polygon is assigned.

## Frame and chronology

The phrase “Later Mayan City-States” compresses a politically heterogeneous world.

Ringle and Bey's synthesis of northern Yucatán cautions against exaggerated territorial
claims for Chichén Itzá, Uxmal and Mayapán. They argue that political influence and
client networks can be broader than effective direct control, and that later traditions
may conflate institutions or sites of different periods.

Mayapán itself is securely relevant to the anchor:
- settlement/nucleation accelerated around 1200 and continued through roughly 1320;
- retrospective historical intervals include “terror and war” around 1302–1323;
- the site remained an important political center until its fifteenth-century collapse.

Therefore Mayapán can function as an in-period member-locus of the aggregate, but not
as a metonym for every Maya polity.

## Mayapán warfare, captives and slave trade

Masson and Peraza Lope's specialist synthesis of Mayapán states that warfare was
endemic and that obtaining war captives for the slave market was likely an important
objective. Their discussion also distinguishes:
- captives taken in war;
- captives destined for slavery or sacrifice;
- military service required from subjects;
- wider mercantile exchange in which slaves could be traded.

The source base is mixed. Much of the explicit “sold into slavery” language comes from
retrospective sixteenth-century historical traditions assembled by Roys and Tozzer.
Those traditions are historically valuable but are not contemporary 1300 ledgers.

The packet therefore treats the slave-trade claim as **specialist historical synthesis
of retrospective evidence**, strengthened by independent archaeological evidence for
warfare/captive-taking but not duplicated by it.

## Archaeological control: violence is not automatically slavery

Mayapán's archaeology documents:
- violent trauma;
- trophy taking;
- sacrificial deposits;
- mass graves;
- political conflict;
- militarized architecture and material culture.

Kennett and colleagues combine radiocarbon/archaeological evidence with retrospective
historical records to test the chronology of violent episodes.

These data materially strengthen the existence and timing of warfare/captive violence.
They do **not** prove that every victim was enslaved, sold or held as property.

The distinction matters because otherwise one could turn every captive body or
sacrificial burial into a slave attestation.

## Corvée / military service versus slavery

Mayapán's political system also demanded personal military service from subjects.
Retrospective reports describe vassals serving “with their persons and arms” when war
was required.

That is coerced/obligatory service in a political relationship. It is not the same
status as p'entak/munach slavery.

EXP-06 therefore preserves:
- slave/captive-sale practice;
- war captivity;
- sacrificial captivity;
- military/corvée obligation;
as related but non-identical forms of coercion.

## Historical terminology

Later/contact-period Yucatec political and lexical sources record terms including:
- **p'entak / ppentac** — male slave/captive in later Yucatec documentation;
- **munach** — female slave/captive/servile status in later documentation;
- ordinary commoner/tributary service categories distinct from slave status.

Wanyerka's political synthesis, following Roys and Yucatec dictionaries, places
p'entak and munach at the bottom of the later Yucatec social hierarchy and connects
slave status to war capture or punishment.

These terms are useful for category control but are **later textual evidence**. They
are not simply inserted into a hypothetical 1300 Mayapán vocabulary without source
qualification.

## Wider Maya evidence and counterweight

Evidence of captive-taking is widespread in Maya history, especially in elite warfare
imagery. But captive status, sacrifice and slavery are not synonyms.

Sabloff's archaeological review notes that ancient Maya slavery has often been
proposed but remains difficult to identify definitively from archaeology alone. That
caution is compatible with the stronger Postclassic Mayapán historical synthesis:
retrospective written sources can support a slave-sale interpretation where bones,
figurines or captive imagery alone cannot.

Earlier Classic/Jaina figurine arguments about slavery are not used as 1300 evidence.

## Law / practice

No comprehensive Mayapán slave code dated to 1300 was recovered.

The strongest evidence concerns reported practice:
- war capture;
- sale/trade of captives;
- military service obligations;
- sacrificial use of captives.

Later/contact-period social terminology and provincial organization provide context,
not an exact legal code for the anchor.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Evidence locus:
- Mayapán and its Yucatecan political/trade network;
- retrospective historical traditions about Mayapán and northern Yucatán;
- archaeological evidence at Mayapán.

Maximum defensible inference:

**A Postclassic Maya polity at Mayapán around the 1300 anchor participated in a
political economy in which warfare produced captives and specialist historical
synthesis supports sale into slavery; archaeology independently supports the warfare
and captive-violence context. This cannot be generalized to all Later Maya city-states.**

Required abstention:
- no aggregate-wide uniform slavery classification;
- no slave count from mass graves, sacrifice or captive imagery;
- no assumption that every war captive was sold;
- no equation of military/corvée service with slave status;
- no back-projection of sixteenth-century provincial boundaries/terminology;
- no prevalence/intensity estimate from Mayapán's unusually rich archaeology.

## Geometry state

**aggregate unresolved; Mayapán point/site is a reference locus only.**

Do not fill all Maya regions from one Mayapán result. Nor should Mayapán's claimed
confederacy/influence network be rendered as direct political control without
year-specific review.

## Source dependency and access audit

- Masson/Peraza Lope's *Kukulcan's Realm* chapters reuse Roys, Tozzer and related
  colonial/ethnohistorical traditions for explicit slave-market claims.
- Their archaeological observations add a different evidence class for warfare and
  violence but do not independently prove slave legal status.
- Kennett et al. combine Mayapán archaeology/radiocarbon data with the same broad
  retrospective chronology; their archaeological chronology is independent, while
  historical episode labels remain dependent on earlier traditions.
- Wanyerka's p'entak/munach social terminology depends largely on Roys/contact-period
  Yucatec sources.
- Ringle/Bey provide a separate specialist critique of Postclassic political extent.
- Sabloff provides an independent archaeology-wide caution about identifying slavery
  from material evidence.

English-language archaeological/ethnohistorical scholarship was reviewed. No
independent reading of original sixteenth-century Yucatec manuscripts or Classic/
Postclassic Maya glyphic corpora was performed.

## Adversarial findings

- **REJECT:** Mayapán = all Later Maya city-states.
- **REJECT:** a captive or sacrificial victim is automatically a slave.
- **REJECT:** personal military service is equivalent to slavery.
- **REJECT:** sixteenth-century p'entak/munach terminology is an exact 1300 lexicon.
- **REJECT:** rich Mayapán archaeology establishes high slavery prevalence.
- **SUPPORTED, bounded:** Postclassic Mayapán around the anchor has defensible
  historical evidence for captive-taking and slave sale/trade.
- **AGGREGATE LIMIT:** no uniform polity-aggregate practice extent is inferred.
- **PORTABLE CONTRACT SURVIVES:** one positive locus can coexist with aggregate
  non-uniformity and retrospective-source limits without schema extension.
