## Bounded synthesis

This case was selected as a deliberate scope-transfer attack against EXP-04's positive
Angkor-node result around 1200.

The attack does **not** require transferring the 1186/1191 temple-inscription evidence
forward a century. Zhou Daguan independently observed Angkor in 1296–1297, essentially
at the 1300 anchor, and devoted a specific section of his account to household slaves.
His description includes purchase, monetary/value equivalence, domestic service,
runaways, punishment and status inherited through a slave mother.

This supports a strong bounded conclusion:

**slavery was an active household institution in the Khmer imperial capital/core
immediately before 1300.**

What does not follow is a uniform empire-wide practice or prevalence claim. Zhou's
observation is concentrated on Angkor and is filtered through an outsider's social and
ethnic categories. Earlier Angkorian inscriptions independently establish long-lived
slave/dependent-property institutions, but they are chronologically earlier and cannot
be used to paint the entire 1300 polity at one intensity.

No P-level is assigned.

## Exact-anchor source: Zhou Daguan, 1296–1297

Zhou Daguan stayed at Angkor for roughly eleven months in 1296–1297 during the reign
of Indravarman III. Peter Harris's direct-from-Chinese translation preserves a chapter
on slaves.

Zhou reports household slaves whom he characterizes as purchased 'savages' from
mountain regions. His account describes:
- purchase/value distinctions between stronger and weaker slaves;
- domestic service and spatial/social segregation within the household;
- corporal punishment;
- runaways who could be tattooed or shackled after recapture;
- children born to enslaved women becoming the master's slaves.

Those details make this much more than a generic servant reference. Within Zhou's
account, the described people form a strongly unfree, commodified and inheritable
status category.

However, several controls are necessary:
- Zhou is one foreign observer;
- the surviving text is incomplete and textually transmitted through later editions;
- his label for the people from whom slaves were purchased is an outsider category,
  not a modern ethnicity/nationality classification;
- his statements about how many slaves 'most families' owned are observational
  generalizations, not a census;
- direct observation was centered on Angkor/Yasodharapura and its social world.

Therefore Zhou is decisive for **presence and household institution at the capital**
but not for empire-wide prevalence.

## Earlier Angkorian epigraphy as continuity/context, not exact-anchor duplication

EXP-04 already established that inscriptions such as K.273 (1186) and K.908 (1191),
plus wider Angkorian personnel corpora, document institutional bonded/unfree labor and
specific slavery/property contexts while warning that not every khñum/personnel entry
is a slave.

Those sources matter here only as predecessor institutional context:
- they show that slavery/dependency was not invented by Zhou's translation;
- they demonstrate multiple source-native categories and long-standing temple/estate
  personnel systems;
- they are roughly a century earlier and must not be counted as separate exact-1300
  observations.

The Khmer 1300 conclusion stands independently on Zhou for near-exact anchor presence.

## khñum and the danger of universal slave translation

Old Khmer scholarship has long debated how to translate **khñum / kñūm**.

Jacob's work on pre-Angkorian inscriptions and later specialist discussion emphasize
that personnel called khñum performed diverse roles and that 'slave' can be too narrow
or misleading when applied automatically. Lustig and Lustig likewise show substantial
status heterogeneity within Angkorian inscriptional personnel.

At the same time, specialist legal-historical work such as Chakravarti documents
clear Cambodian slavery pathways including capture in war, birth, punishment,
mortgage/pledge, gift, inheritance and purchase in different periods/sources.

The correct mapping is therefore claim-specific:
- Zhou's late-thirteenth-century household category is strongly slave-like by the
  concrete property/runaway/hereditary features he describes;
- khñum in inscriptions is not universally normalized to slave;
- dāsa/dāsī and other status labels remain source/context dependent;
- temple personnel totals are not slave totals.

## Acquisition and 'mountain people'

Zhou says the household slaves he describes were purchased from people he viewed as
wild/mountain populations and adds a separate section about such groups.

This is evidence for an acquisition/network dimension: people from outside or at the
margins of the Angkorian lowland core were being commodified into household slavery.

It does **not** authorize:
- identifying those people with one modern ethnic group;
- inferring that all upland peoples were slaves;
- mapping every supposed source region as practicing slavery;
- treating the buyer's location as the place of original capture.

Origin identity, capture location, sale location and place of slaveholding remain
separate claims.

## Prevalence: Zhou's numbers are not a P-level

Zhou makes striking statements about slaveholding frequency and household numbers.

Those claims are historically important because they show that he perceived slavery
as common and socially visible. They are not a population survey.

EXP-06 therefore preserves Zhou's prevalence impression as a **source claim** but does
not convert it mechanically into:
- a polity-wide percentage;
- a labor-share estimate;
- P3/P4;
- a map intensity.

Earlier inscription/personnel counts are likewise not combined with Zhou's household
numbers to manufacture prevalence.

## Law / practice

The exact-anchor evidence is principally practice/observation, not a surviving 1300
slave code.

Earlier Khmer legal/epigraphic scholarship documents slaves as property-bearing/legal
categories and multiple routes into slavery. That supplies institutional history but
not proof of one uniform law enforced everywhere in 1300.

Zhou's observed treatment of slaves is therefore kept distinct from earlier juridical
reconstruction.

## Network / territorial separation

Positive dimensions:
- household slavery at Angkor immediately before 1300;
- acquisition/purchase connections to people beyond the capital core;
- longer Angkorian institutional continuity from earlier epigraphy.

Unresolved dimensions:
- uniformity across the full Khmer Empire;
- exact source regions and capture mechanisms for Zhou's purchased slaves;
- polity-wide prevalence/intensity;
- practice in every provincial/local jurisdiction.

The capital-core result must not become a filled empire polygon.

## Evidence locus and inference extent

Primary evidence locus:
- Angkor/Yasodharapura, 1296–1297.

Contextual/predecessor loci:
- earlier Angkorian temple/institutional inscription network;
- wider old-Khmer legal/social evidence discussed by specialist scholarship.

Maximum defensible inference:

**slavery was an active, commodified household institution in the Khmer imperial
capital/core immediately before 1300, independently attested by Zhou Daguan; earlier
epigraphy demonstrates deeper institutional continuity. The evidence does not justify
a uniform empire-wide practice level or prevalence estimate.**

Required abstention:
- no all-empire slavery polygon;
- no P-level from Zhou's reported household numbers;
- no universal khñum = slave mapping;
- no modern ethnicity inference from Zhou's 'mountain/savage' category;
- no assumption that place of purchase equals place of capture/enslavement;
- no double-counting EXP-04's K.273/K.908 chains as new 1300 attestations.

## Geometry state

**capital/core locus supported; polity-wide practice geometry unresolved.**

Angkor is a defensible near-anchor evidence locus. The political extent of the Khmer
Empire at 1300 requires separate historical geometry review and cannot inherit one
practice value from the capital.

## Source dependency and access audit

- Zhou Daguan's 1296–1297 account is one eyewitness textual chain. Harris 2007 is the
  preferred modern direct-from-Chinese English translation used here.
- older English versions mediated through Pelliot's French translation are not
  independent eyewitness sources.
- publisher descriptions, reviews and modern quotations of Zhou are metadata/
  interpretation layers on the same primary text.
- K.273/K.908 and Lustig/Lustig belong to the earlier Angkorian epigraphic evidence
  already audited in EXP-04; they are predecessor continuity/context here.
- Jacob and related khñum scholarship are category-interpretation layers over the
  epigraphic corpus, not new slavery events.
- Chakravarti's slavery synthesis uses inscriptions and Chinese records across long
  periods; its value is acquisition/legal structure, not a new 1300 observation.

Peter Harris's English translation, specialist Old Khmer scholarship and accessible
epigraphic/legal syntheses were reviewed. No independent Classical Chinese or Old
Khmer critical edition was produced in EXP-06.

## Adversarial findings

- **SURVIVES:** the 1300 Khmer polity target has independent near-exact anchor slavery
  evidence at its capital; the result does not depend on forward-projecting Angkor 1200.
- **REJECT:** Zhou's household numbers establish empire-wide prevalence or P4.
- **REJECT:** khñum universally means slave.
- **REJECT:** people Zhou calls mountain/savage can be assigned a modern nationality
  or mapped wholesale as slave-source territory.
- **REJECT:** one capital-core observation colors the entire Khmer Empire.
- **REJECT:** K.273/K.908 become additional 1300 attestations.
- **PORTABLE CONTRACT SURVIVES:** exact-anchor capital presence, source prevalence
  claims, acquisition network and polity-wide abstention remain separable without
  schema extension.
